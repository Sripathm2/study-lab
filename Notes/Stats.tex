Definition / Intuition / Notes form. Written to stand alone --- no book
required to read them. Math is kept light: a one-line formula plus plain
English, not derivations.

Split out from the ML notes to keep the statistics track separate
(companion to StatQuest). Machine-learning and deep-learning methods ---
trees, SVMs, neural networks, transformers, unsupervised learning, and
ML systems design --- live in a companion \textbf{ML\_notes.md}, and
cross-references to them are marked \emph{(ML)}.

Sources here: \emph{An Introduction to Statistical Learning} (ISL/ISLP)
and StatQuest (Josh Starmer), with standard additions marked inline.

\hypertarget{statistical-learning-foundations-isl-ch.-2}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Statistical Learning --- Foundations
\emph{(ISL
ch.~2)}}{Statistical Learning --- Foundations (ISL ch.~2)}}\label{statistical-learning-foundations-isl-ch.-2}}

\hypertarget{statistical-learning}{%
\subsubsection{Statistical learning}\label{statistical-learning}}

\textbf{Definition.} A set of approaches for estimating an unknown
function \texttt{f} that links inputs \texttt{X\ =\ (X\_1,\ …,\ X\_p)}
to an output \texttt{Y}, modeled as \texttt{Y\ =\ f(X)\ +\ ε}, where
\texttt{ε} is a random error term independent of \texttt{X} with mean
zero.

\textbf{Intuition.} We assume some systematic relationship \texttt{f}
connects inputs to output, but it's buried in noise. Statistical
learning is the toolkit for recovering as much of \texttt{f} as the data
allows --- either to predict new outputs, or to understand how the
inputs drive the output.

\textbf{Notes.} \texttt{ε} is the \emph{irreducible error} (→ Reducible
vs irreducible error). If every observation pairs inputs with a known
\texttt{Y}, the problem is \emph{supervised}; with no \texttt{Y}, it's
\emph{unsupervised}. Everything downstream --- parametric vs
non-parametric, the bias--variance trade-off --- is about how well we
can pin down \texttt{}.

\hypertarget{supervised-vs-unsupervised-learning}{%
\subsubsection{Supervised vs unsupervised
learning}\label{supervised-vs-unsupervised-learning}}

\textbf{Definition.} Supervised learning fits a model from observations
that each pair predictors \texttt{x\_i} with a response \texttt{y\_i},
for prediction or inference. Unsupervised learning has predictors
\texttt{x\_i} but no response \texttt{y\_i}, so the goal is to find
structure among observations rather than predict a labeled output.

\textbf{Intuition.} Supervised = learning with an answer key: every
example tells you the right output, so you can measure and correct
error. Unsupervised = working blind: no answer key, so you look for
patterns, groupings, or structure in the inputs themselves.

\textbf{Notes.} Supervised methods here: linear regression, logistic
regression, LDA/QDA, trees, GAMs, boosting, SVMs, most neural nets.
Unsupervised can't fit a regression model --- there's no response to
supervise it. Almost all model-accuracy machinery presumes a response to
compare against. → Unsupervised learning (ML) (ch.~12), Semi-supervised
learning.

\hypertarget{semi-supervised-learning}{%
\subsubsection{Semi-supervised
learning}\label{semi-supervised-learning}}

\textbf{Definition.} A setting with \texttt{n} observations where
\texttt{m\ \textless{}\ n} have both predictors and a response, and the
remaining \texttt{n\ −\ m} have predictors only; the aim is a method
that uses both.

\textbf{Intuition.} Common when predictors are cheap to measure but
responses are expensive to collect. You don't want to discard the
unlabeled majority, so you use them to sharpen an estimate anchored by
the labeled few.

\textbf{Notes.} Sits between supervised and unsupervised. \emph{(beyond
ISL ch.~2 --- named but not developed there.)}

\hypertarget{prediction-vs-inference}{%
\subsubsection{Prediction vs inference}\label{prediction-vs-inference}}

\textbf{Definition.} In prediction, the goal is an accurate \texttt{}
giving good output estimates \texttt{Ŷ\ =\ (X)} for new inputs, and
\texttt{} may be a black box. In inference, the goal is to understand
the relationship between \texttt{X} and \texttt{Y} --- which predictors
matter, in what direction, how --- so \texttt{} must be interpretable.

\textbf{Intuition.} Prediction only cares that the answer is right; you
needn't know why. Inference cares about the ``why,'' and will trade some
predictive accuracy for a model you can read.

\textbf{Notes.} This goal drives model choice: prediction can justify
flexible black boxes; inference favors restrictive, interpretable models
like linear regression. → Flexibility vs interpretability.

\hypertarget{reducible-vs-irreducible-error}{%
\subsubsection{Reducible vs irreducible
error}\label{reducible-vs-irreducible-error}}

\textbf{Definition.} For \texttt{Ŷ\ =\ (X)}, expected squared
prediction error splits as
\texttt{E{[}(Y\ −\ Ŷ)²{]}\ =\ {[}f(X)\ −\ (X){]}²\ +\ Var(ε)}. The
first term is \emph{reducible error} (shrinks as \texttt{} improves);
\texttt{Var(ε)} is the \emph{irreducible error}, a floor no model can
remove.

\textbf{Intuition.} Some error is your model's fault (a wrong or
imprecise \texttt{}) --- you can chip away at it with better methods
and more data. The rest is baked into the problem: unmeasured variables,
inherent randomness. Even the true \texttt{f} wouldn't predict
perfectly.

\textbf{Notes.} \emph{Emphasized:} you can only ever attack the
reducible part. \texttt{Var(ε)} reappears as the noise floor in the
Bias--variance trade-off, and it's why test error bottoms out above
zero. Its classification analogue is the Bayes error rate.

\hypertarget{quantitative-vs-qualitative-variables}{%
\subsubsection{Quantitative vs qualitative
variables}\label{quantitative-vs-qualitative-variables}}

\textbf{Definition.} Quantitative variables take numerical values
(e.g.~salary, age). Qualitative (categorical) variables take values in
one of \texttt{K} classes or categories (e.g.~brand, disease status).

\textbf{Intuition.} Numbers you can average vs labels you can only
count. The type of the \emph{response} decides what kind of problem you
have.

\textbf{Notes.} Quantitative response → regression; qualitative response
→ classification. → Regression vs classification.

\hypertarget{regression-vs-classification}{%
\subsubsection{Regression vs
classification}\label{regression-vs-classification}}

\textbf{Definition.} Problems with a quantitative response are
\emph{regression} problems; problems with a qualitative response are
\emph{classification} problems.

\textbf{Intuition.} Predicting ``how much / how many'' (a number) is
regression; predicting ``which class'' (a label) is classification.

\textbf{Notes.} The line isn't crisp. Logistic regression is a
classification method but estimates class probabilities, so it has a
regression flavor. Some methods --- KNN, boosting --- handle either
response type. Two classification methods for qualitative responses
covered later: Logistic regression (models the log-odds directly) and
Linear discriminant analysis (models each class's distribution, then
applies Bayes' rule) --- distinct methods, both detailed in the
Classification section.

\hypertarget{estimating-f-isl-ch.-2}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Estimating f \emph{(ISL
ch.~2)}}{Estimating f (ISL ch.~2)}}\label{estimating-f-isl-ch.-2}}

\hypertarget{parametric-methods}{%
\subsubsection{Parametric methods}\label{parametric-methods}}

\textbf{Definition.} A two-step, model-based approach to estimating
\texttt{f}: (1) assume a functional form for \texttt{f} (e.g.~linear,
\texttt{f(X)\ =\ β\_0\ +\ β\_1\ X\_1\ +\ …\ +\ β\_p\ X\_p}); (2) use
training data to fit/train the model --- i.e.~estimate its parameters
(for the linear form, the \texttt{p\ +\ 1} coefficients).

\textbf{Intuition.} Rather than searching all possible functions, commit
to a shape and tune its dials. Assuming linearity collapses an arbitrary
\texttt{p}-dimensional function into just \texttt{p\ +\ 1} numbers to
estimate --- far easier, and less data-hungry.

\textbf{Notes.} The risk: if the assumed form is wrong, \texttt{} stays
biased no matter how much data you have. Advantages: easy to fit, few
coefficients, interpretable, easy significance tests. The linear form is
typically fit by least squares. Contrast Non-parametric methods.

\hypertarget{non-parametric-methods}{%
\subsubsection{Non-parametric methods}\label{non-parametric-methods}}

\textbf{Definition.} Methods that make no explicit assumption about the
functional form of \texttt{f}; they seek an \texttt{} that gets as
close to the data as possible without being too rough or wiggly.

\textbf{Intuition.} You don't pre-commit to a shape --- you let the data
draw \texttt{f}. This can capture relationships a linear model would
miss.

\textbf{Notes.} Cost: with no small parameter set to pin down, you need
far more observations to estimate \texttt{f} accurately. You trade
freedom from a possibly-wrong assumption for data-hunger and overfitting
risk. KNN is the flagship example. Contrast Parametric methods.

\hypertarget{overfitting}{%
\subsubsection{Overfitting}\label{overfitting}}

\textbf{Definition.} When a model follows the training data's noise too
closely, producing a small training error but a large test error.

\textbf{Intuition.} The model memorizes quirks of the training sample
--- noise that won't recur --- instead of the underlying signal. Great
on data it has seen, poor on data it hasn't.

\textbf{Notes.} More flexible methods are more prone to it. It's why the
test-error curve is U-shaped: past a point, added flexibility buys
noise-fitting, not signal. In bias--variance terms, overfitting = low
bias but high variance. (A modern wrinkle: past the \emph{interpolation}
point test error can fall again --- see Double descent.) →
Bias--variance trade-off, Training MSE vs test MSE.

\hypertarget{flexibility-vs-interpretability}{%
\subsubsection{Flexibility vs
interpretability}\label{flexibility-vs-interpretability}}

\textbf{Definition.} Methods trade off along a spectrum from
inflexible/interpretable to flexible/opaque. Least squares linear
regression is inflexible but highly interpretable; the lasso is similar;
GAMs are more flexible while staying fairly interpretable; bagging,
boosting, SVMs with non-linear kernels, and neural nets are highly
flexible but hard to interpret.

\textbf{Intuition.} Flexibility = how many shapes a method can bend to.
More flexibility can fit complex truths but makes the model harder to
read and easier to overfit. Want to explain the \texttt{X}--\texttt{Y}
relationship (inference)? Reach for a restrictive, readable model.

\textbf{Notes.} \emph{Emphasized:} more flexible ≠ more accurate.
Because of overfitting, a less flexible method often predicts better on
test data. Choosing the right level of flexibility is the central
practical problem, in both regression and classification. → Prediction
vs inference, Bias--variance trade-off, Neural networks.

\hypertarget{assessing-model-accuracy-isl-ch.-2}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Assessing Model Accuracy \emph{(ISL
ch.~2)}}{Assessing Model Accuracy (ISL ch.~2)}}\label{assessing-model-accuracy-isl-ch.-2}}

\hypertarget{mean-squared-error-mse}{%
\subsubsection{Mean squared error (MSE)}\label{mean-squared-error-mse}}

\textbf{Definition.} In regression, the standard measure of fit quality:
\texttt{MSE\ =\ (1/n)\ Σ\ (y\_i\ −\ (x\_i))²} --- the average squared
gap between observed responses and predictions.

\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{MSE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(y_i-\hat f(x_i)\bigr)^2
\qquad\text{(train MSE on the fitting set, test MSE on held-out data)}
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} How far off predictions are on average, with big
misses penalized disproportionately (squaring). Small MSE = predictions
land close to the truth.

\textbf{Notes.} On the fitting data it's \emph{training MSE}; what
matters is \emph{test MSE} on unseen data. → Training MSE vs test MSE.

\hypertarget{training-mse-vs-test-mse}{%
\subsubsection{Training MSE vs test
MSE}\label{training-mse-vs-test-mse}}

\textbf{Definition.} Training MSE is computed on the data used to fit
the model; test MSE on previously unseen data. The objective is to
minimize test MSE.

\textbf{Intuition.} Acing questions you studied (training) doesn't prove
you'll ace the exam (test). Only fresh data measures real predictive
skill.

\textbf{Notes.} \emph{Emphasized:} no guarantee the lowest-training-MSE
method has the lowest test MSE --- often it's the reverse. As
flexibility rises, training MSE falls \emph{monotonically} while test
MSE traces a \emph{U-shape} (falls, then rises as overfitting sets in).
A widening gap is the signature of overfitting. When no test set exists,
resampling estimates test error → Cross-validation (Resampling Methods).

\hypertarget{biasvariance-trade-off}{%
\subsubsection{Bias--variance trade-off}\label{biasvariance-trade-off}}

\textbf{Definition.} Expected test MSE at a point \texttt{x\_0}
decomposes as
\texttt{E{[}(y\_0\ −\ (x\_0))²{]}\ =\ Var((x\_0))\ +\ {[}Bias((x\_0)){]}²\ +\ Var(ε)}.
Minimizing expected test error requires \emph{simultaneously} low
variance and low bias; as flexibility changes, the two typically move in
opposite directions.

\begin{equation*}
\mathbb{E}\bigl[(y_0-\hat f(x_0))^2\bigr]
=\underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(\hat f(x_0))}_{\text{variance}}
+\underbrace{\bigl[\operatorname{Bias}(\hat f(x_0))\bigr]^2}_{\text{bias}^2}
+\underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon)}_{\text{irreducible}}
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} Expected test error has three ingredients: how much
the fit jumps around across different training sets (\emph{variance}),
how much the model's simplifying assumptions distort the truth
(\emph{bias²}), and the irreducible noise floor (\texttt{Var(ε)}). You
can't zero out both bias and variance at once --- tighten one and the
other usually loosens --- so you aim for the sweet spot that minimizes
their sum. As flexibility rises, bias falls but variance rises. The
U-shaped test-MSE curve \emph{is} this trade-off made visible.

\textbf{Notes.} \texttt{Var(ε)} is the irreducible error; expected test
MSE can never drop below it. Underfitting = high bias; overfitting =
high variance. This trade-off touches nearly every method here.
\emph{Modern caveat (ch.~10):} it describes behavior up to the point of
interpolation; push flexibility further and test error can descend a
second time --- see Double descent. → Bias, Variance, Overfitting.

\hypertarget{bias}{%
\subsubsection{Bias}\label{bias}}

\textbf{Definition.} The error introduced by approximating a complicated
real-world relationship with a much simpler model. As methods get more
flexible, bias generally decreases.

\textbf{Intuition.} Force a straight line through a curvy truth and
you're systematically wrong in a way more data won't fix --- that's
bias. Simpler, more restrictive models carry more of it.

\textbf{Notes.} Low bias is one of the two things low test error needs;
the other is low Variance. High bias = underfitting. Trades off against
Variance.

\hypertarget{variance}{%
\subsubsection{Variance}\label{variance}}

\textbf{Definition.} The amount by which \texttt{} would change if
estimated on a different training set. More flexible methods generally
have higher variance.

\textbf{Intuition.} Refit on a fresh sample from the same population ---
does the fit barely move, or swing wildly? Wild swings = high variance.
Flexible methods bend to each sample's noise, so they're less stable.

\textbf{Notes.} \emph{Emphasized:} small changes in training data
producing large changes in \texttt{} is the hallmark of high variance.
High variance = overfitting. Trades off against Bias. Bagging exists
specifically to reduce variance.

\hypertarget{classification-error-rate}{%
\subsubsection{Classification error
rate}\label{classification-error-rate}}

\textbf{Definition.} In classification, fit quality is measured by the
error rate --- the fraction of misclassified observations. The
\emph{test error rate} on test observations \texttt{(x\_0,\ y\_0)} is
\texttt{Ave(I(y\_0\ ≠\ ŷ\_0))}, the average of the indicator that the
predicted label differs from the true one; the \emph{training error
rate} is the same on training data.

\textbf{Intuition.} The classification analogue of MSE --- instead of
averaging squared numeric misses, you count how often you named the
wrong class.

\textbf{Notes.} \texttt{I(·)} is the indicator function (1 if true, else
0). As with MSE, training error falls with flexibility while test error
is U-shaped. A good classifier has a small \emph{test} error rate; its
theoretical floor is the Bayes error rate.

\hypertarget{bayes-classifier}{%
\subsubsection{Bayes classifier}\label{bayes-classifier}}

\textbf{Definition.} The classifier that assigns each observation with
predictors \texttt{x\_0} to the most probable class given those
predictors --- the class \texttt{j} maximizing
\texttt{Pr(Y\ =\ j\ \textbar{}\ X\ =\ x\_0)}. Two-class: predict class 1
if \texttt{Pr(Y\ =\ 1\ \textbar{}\ X\ =\ x\_0)\ \textgreater{}\ 0.5},
else class 2.

\begin{equation*}
\hat y(x_0)=\arg\max_{j}\;\Pr(Y=j\mid X=x_0)
\qquad
\text{Bayes error}=1-\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\max_j \Pr(Y=j\mid X)\Bigr]
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} If you knew the true class probabilities at every
point, your best bet is always the most likely class. That's the Bayes
classifier --- the gold standard every real classifier tries to
approximate.

\textbf{Notes.} In practice we don't know the true conditional
distribution and must estimate it --- KNN estimates it locally;
LDA/QDA/naive Bayes estimate it via Bayes' theorem. Produces the lowest
possible test error rate (→ Bayes error rate) and defines the Bayes
decision boundary.

\hypertarget{bayes-decision-boundary}{%
\subsubsection{Bayes decision boundary}\label{bayes-decision-boundary}}

\textbf{Definition.} The set of points where the Bayes classifier is
indifferent between classes --- two-class: where
\texttt{Pr(Y\ =\ 1\ \textbar{}\ X\ =\ x\_0)\ =\ 0.5}. It partitions the
predictor space into per-class regions.

\textbf{Intuition.} The dividing line the perfect classifier would draw.
One side, one class wins; the other side, the other.

\textbf{Notes.} Real classifiers try to approximate it. A too-flexible
fit (KNN with \texttt{K\ =\ 1}) yields a jagged boundary that chases
noise instead of tracking the true boundary.

\hypertarget{bayes-error-rate}{%
\subsubsection{Bayes error rate}\label{bayes-error-rate}}

\textbf{Definition.} The lowest possible test error rate, achieved by
the Bayes classifier. Overall it equals
\texttt{1\ −\ E{[}max\_j\ Pr(Y\ =\ j\ \textbar{}\ X){]}} (expectation
over \texttt{X}); at a single point \texttt{x\_0} it is
\texttt{1\ −\ max\_j\ Pr(Y\ =\ j\ \textbar{}\ X\ =\ x\_0)}.

\textbf{Intuition.} Even the perfect classifier is wrong sometimes,
because classes overlap --- at some \texttt{x}, more than one class
carries real probability. The Bayes error rate is that unavoidable
minimum.

\textbf{Notes.} The classification analogue of irreducible error --- a
floor no classifier beats.

\hypertarget{k-nearest-neighbors-knn}{%
\subsubsection{K-nearest neighbors
(KNN)}\label{k-nearest-neighbors-knn}}

\textbf{Definition.} A non-parametric classifier. Given \texttt{K} and a
test point \texttt{x\_0}, KNN finds the \texttt{K} closest training
points (\texttt{N\_0}), estimates each class's probability as the
fraction of \texttt{N\_0} in that class, and assigns \texttt{x\_0} to
the most probable class.

\begin{equation*}
\Pr(Y=j\mid X=x_0)\;\approx\;\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}_0}\mathbb{1}(y_i=j),
\qquad \mathcal{N}_0=\text{the }K\text{ training points nearest }x_0
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} Ask the \texttt{K} nearest neighbors to vote; go
with the majority. It approximates the Bayes classifier by estimating
the true probabilities locally from whoever's nearby.

\textbf{Notes.} \emph{Emphasized:} the choice of \texttt{K} matters a
lot, and \texttt{1/K} acts as the flexibility knob. \texttt{K\ =\ 1} is
maximally flexible --- jagged, low-bias/high-variance, chasing noise;
large \texttt{K} is smoother, higher-bias/lower-variance. Choosing
\texttt{K} well \emph{is} the bias--variance trade-off in action. KNN
also does regression → KNN regression. → Non-parametric methods, Bayes
classifier.

\begin{center}\rule{0.5\linewidth}{0.5pt}\end{center}

\hypertarget{linear-regression-isl-ch.-3}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Linear Regression \emph{(ISL
ch.~3)}}{Linear Regression (ISL ch.~3)}}\label{linear-regression-isl-ch.-3}}

\hypertarget{simple-linear-regression}{%
\subsubsection{Simple linear
regression}\label{simple-linear-regression}}

\textbf{Definition.} Predicting a quantitative response \texttt{Y} from
a single predictor \texttt{X} by assuming a straight-line relationship:
\texttt{Y\ ≈\ β\_0\ +\ β\_1\ X}, where \texttt{β\_0} is the intercept
and \texttt{β\_1} the slope. Fitting this is ``regressing \texttt{Y} on
\texttt{X}.''

\begin{equation*}
Y=\beta_0+\beta_1X+\varepsilon,
\qquad
\hat\beta_1=\frac{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2},
\qquad
\hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} Draw the best straight line through a scatter of
points. \texttt{β\_1} says how much \texttt{Y} moves per one-unit rise
in \texttt{X}; \texttt{β\_0} is where the line crosses when
\texttt{X\ =\ 0}.

\textbf{Notes.} The simplest parametric model. Extends to many
predictors → Multiple linear regression. Coefficients are estimated by
least squares.

\hypertarget{least-squares-and-residuals-rss}{%
\subsubsection{Least squares and residuals
(RSS)}\label{least-squares-and-residuals-rss}}

\textbf{Definition.} The \texttt{i}-th \emph{residual} is
\texttt{e\_i\ =\ y\_i\ −\ ŷ\_i}, the gap between the observed and
predicted response. The \emph{residual sum of squares} is
\texttt{RSS\ =\ e\_1²\ +\ …\ +\ e\_n²}. The \emph{least squares} fit
chooses \texttt{\_0,\ \_1} to minimize RSS.

\begin{equation*}
e_i=y_i-\hat y_i,
\qquad
\mathrm{RSS}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_i^2=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(y_i-\hat\beta_0-\hat\beta_1x_i\bigr)^2
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} Each residual is a miss; squaring makes big misses
count more and keeps signs from cancelling. Least squares picks the line
that makes the total squared miss as small as possible --- the line that
``splits the difference'' best.

\textbf{Notes.} For simple regression there are closed-form solutions
for \texttt{\_0} and \texttt{\_1} in terms of the sample means and the
spread of \texttt{X}. RSS also drives R², the F-statistic, and tree
splitting. → R-squared and correlation.

\hypertarget{population-regression-line-vs-least-squares-line}{%
\subsubsection{Population regression line vs least squares
line}\label{population-regression-line-vs-least-squares-line}}

\textbf{Definition.} The \emph{population regression line} is the true
best-fit line \texttt{Y\ =\ β\_0\ +\ β\_1\ X\ +\ ε} for the whole
population (unobserved). The \emph{least squares line} is the estimate
\texttt{ŷ\ =\ \_0\ +\ \_1\ x} computed from one sample.

\textbf{Intuition.} Same idea as estimating a population mean from a
sample mean: on one sample your line sits a little high, on another a
little low, but averaged over many samples it lands on the truth. The
least squares line is an \emph{unbiased} estimate of the population
line.

\textbf{Notes.} The gap between the two is why we need standard errors
and confidence intervals --- to say how far our sample line might be
from the truth.

\hypertarget{standard-error-of-a-coefficient}{%
\subsubsection{Standard error of a
coefficient}\label{standard-error-of-a-coefficient}}

\textbf{Definition.} The standard error \texttt{SE()} measures how much
an estimated coefficient would vary across different samples. For the
sample mean, \texttt{SE()²\ =\ σ²/n}, where \texttt{σ} is the standard
deviation of each observation; analogous formulas give \texttt{SE(\_0)}
and \texttt{SE(\_1)}, with \texttt{σ²\ =\ Var(ε)}.

\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)^2=\frac{\sigma^2}{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2},
\qquad
\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_0)^2=\sigma^2\left[\frac{1}{n}+\frac{\bar x^2}{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2}\right]
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} It's the typical wobble in an estimate. More data
(larger \texttt{n}) and predictors spread over a wider range both shrink
the wobble, giving you a more precise coefficient.

\textbf{Notes.} Standard errors feed confidence intervals and hypothesis
tests. Small \texttt{SE(\_1)} means even a modest \texttt{\_1} is
convincing evidence of a real relationship. → Confidence interval,
Hypothesis test.

\hypertarget{confidence-interval}{%
\subsubsection{Confidence interval}\label{confidence-interval}}

\textbf{Definition.} A range that contains the true parameter with a
stated probability (typically 95\%). For a regression coefficient, the
95\% CI is approximately \texttt{\_1\ ±\ 2·SE(\_1)}; for a mean,
\texttt{\ ±\ 2·SE} with \texttt{SE\ =\ σ/√n}. It can also be read
straight off a bootstrap: the middle 95\% of the bootstrapped estimates.

\textbf{Intuition.} ``We're 95\% confident the truth lies in here.''
More precisely: if you repeated the sampling many times, about 95\% of
the intervals you'd build this way would contain the true value. A value
\emph{outside} the 95\% CI is one the data reject at
\texttt{p\ \textless{}\ 0.05}, so a CI doubles as a visual hypothesis
test.

\textbf{Example.} Bootstrap the mean weight of a sample of mice 10,000
times; the 2.5th--97.5th percentiles of those means, say
\texttt{{[}19.4,\ 21.8{]}\ g}, are the 95\% CI. Since \texttt{18\ g}
lies outside it, the true mean weight is greater than 18 g at
\texttt{p\ \textless{}\ 0.05}. Comparing two groups: if the 95\% CIs for
female and male mouse weight \textbf{don't overlap}, the difference is
significant (\texttt{p\ \textless{}\ 0.05}); if they \textbf{do}
overlap, it's inconclusive --- run a t-test.

\textbf{Notes.} Width scales with the standard error, so more data →
tighter interval. \emph{Caveat on the overlap rule:} non-overlapping CIs
imply a significant difference, but overlapping CIs do \textbf{not}
guarantee non-significance --- hence the follow-up t-test. → Standard
error and the sampling distribution of the mean, The bootstrap, t-tests
(one-sample, paired, two-sample), Standard error of a coefficient.
\emph{(StatQuest)}

\hypertarget{hypothesis-test-t-statistic-and-p-value}{%
\subsubsection{Hypothesis test, t-statistic, and
p-value}\label{hypothesis-test-t-statistic-and-p-value}}

\textbf{Definition.} To test whether \texttt{X} is related to
\texttt{Y}, test the \emph{null hypothesis} \texttt{H\_0:\ β\_1\ =\ 0}
(no relationship) against the \emph{alternative}
\texttt{H\_a:\ β\_1\ ≠\ 0}. Compute the \emph{t-statistic}
\texttt{t\ =\ (\_1\ −\ 0)\ /\ SE(\_1)}, compare it to a
t-distribution, and read off the \emph{p-value}.

\begin{equation*}
t=\frac{\hat\beta_1-0}{\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)}\sim t_{n-2}\ \text{under }H_0:\beta_1=0,
\qquad
p=\Pr\bigl(|T|\ge|t|\bigr)
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} The t-statistic asks: how many standard errors is
our estimate away from zero? Far from zero → unlikely to be a fluke. The
p-value is the probability of seeing an association this strong purely
by chance if there were really no relationship. Small p-value → reject
the null and declare a real relationship.

\textbf{Notes.} Small \texttt{SE(\_1)} makes even small \texttt{\_1}
significant; large SE demands a big \texttt{\_1}. In classification
(logistic regression) the analogue is the z-statistic. ``Significant'' ≠
``large effect.'' (The four-step logic --- hypotheses → test statistic →
p-value → decide --- generalizes to any hypothesis test; you never
``accept'' \texttt{H\_0}, only ``fail to reject'' it.) → Type I and Type
II errors.

\hypertarget{residual-standard-error-rse}{%
\subsubsection{Residual standard error
(RSE)}\label{residual-standard-error-rse}}

\textbf{Definition.} An estimate of the standard deviation of the noise
\texttt{ε} --- roughly, the average amount the response deviates from
the true regression line: \texttt{RSE\ =\ sqrt(RSS\ /\ (n\ −\ 2))} for
simple regression.

\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{RSE}=\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{n-p-1}}
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} Even a perfect model can't predict exactly, because
of \texttt{ε}. RSE says how big those inherent misses are, in the units
of \texttt{Y}. Smaller RSE = tighter fit.

\textbf{Notes.} An \emph{absolute} measure of lack of fit --- but being
in \texttt{Y}'s units, ``good'' is context-dependent, which is why R² (a
unit-free proportion) is often reported alongside. → R-squared and
correlation.

\hypertarget{r-squared-and-correlation}{%
\subsubsection{R-squared and
correlation}\label{r-squared-and-correlation}}

\textbf{Definition.} \texttt{R²\ =\ 1\ −\ RSS/TSS}, where
\texttt{TSS\ =\ Σ(y\_i\ −\ ȳ)²} is the total sum of squares.
Equivalently, the \emph{variance-explained} form:
\texttt{R²\ =\ (SS(mean)\ −\ SS(fit))\ /\ SS(mean)}, where
\texttt{SS(mean)\ =\ Σ(y\_i\ −\ ȳ)²} is the variation around the mean
and \texttt{SS(fit)\ =\ Σ(y\_i\ −\ ŷ\_i)²} the variation around the
fitted line. It's the \emph{proportion of variance in \texttt{Y}
explained} by the model, always between 0 and 1. Correlation
\texttt{Cor(X,\ Y)\ =\ r} measures the linear relationship on
\texttt{{[}−1,\ 1{]}}; in \emph{simple} linear regression,
\texttt{R²\ =\ r²}.

\begin{equation*}
R^2=\frac{\mathrm{TSS}-\mathrm{RSS}}{\mathrm{TSS}}=1-\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{\mathrm{TSS}},
\qquad
\mathrm{TSS}=\sum_i (y_i-\bar y)^2
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} TSS is how much \texttt{Y} varies on its own; RSS is
how much variation is left after the model. Their ratio is the fraction
the model mopped up. Its big advantage over the raw correlation
\texttt{r} is \emph{interpretability}: \texttt{r\ =\ 0.7} vs
\texttt{r\ =\ 0.5} doesn't tell you ``how much better,'' but \texttt{R²}
does --- \texttt{R²\ =\ 0.7} explains \textbf{1.4×} as much variance as
\texttt{R²\ =\ 0.5} (0.7 / 0.5). The cost: \texttt{R²} drops the
\emph{sign}, so unlike \texttt{r} it won't tell you whether the
relationship is positive or negative.

\textbf{Example.} Regress mouse weight on size; suppose the variation
around the mean is \texttt{SS(mean)\ =\ 100} and around the fitted line
\texttt{SS(fit)\ =\ 20}. Then \texttt{R²\ =\ (100\ −\ 20)/100\ =\ 0.80}
--- size explains 80\% of the weight variation, leaving 20\%
unexplained.

\textbf{Notes.} Unit-free, unlike RSE, so easier to interpret across
problems. \emph{Correction to the jotted formula:} it's
\texttt{(SS(mean)\ −\ SS(fit))\ /\ SS(mean)} --- the subtraction sits
\textbf{inside} the numerator (mind the parentheses), and each
\texttt{SS} is a sum of \textbf{squared} deviations
\texttt{Σ(x\_i\ −\ )²}, not \texttt{Σ(x\_i\ −\ )} (which is always 0).
The \texttt{R²\ =\ r²} identity only holds for a single predictor; with
multiple predictors R² generalizes but correlation doesn't directly.
Adding predictors never decreases R² → motivates adjusted R². → Adjusted
R-squared. \emph{(StatQuest)}

\hypertarget{multiple-linear-regression}{%
\subsubsection{Multiple linear
regression}\label{multiple-linear-regression}}

\textbf{Definition.} Extends simple regression to \texttt{p} predictors:
\texttt{Y\ =\ β\_0\ +\ β\_1\ X\_1\ +\ …\ +\ β\_p\ X\_p\ +\ ε}. Each
\texttt{β\_j} is the \emph{average effect on \texttt{Y} of a one-unit
increase in \texttt{X\_j}, holding all other predictors fixed}.
Coefficients are again estimated by least squares (minimizing RSS).

\begin{equation*}
Y=\beta_0+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_jX_j+\varepsilon
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=(\mathbf{X}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{y}
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} Instead of one slope you have one per predictor,
each isolating that predictor's effect while the others are held
constant. That ``holding fixed'' is crucial --- a predictor's solo
effect can differ sharply from its effect alongside correlated others (→
Confounding).

\textbf{Example.} \texttt{price\ =\ 50\ +\ 0.15·size\ −\ 0.8·age} (price
in \$k, size in sq ft, age in years): holding age fixed, each extra
square foot adds \$150; holding size fixed, each extra year subtracts
\$800. The age coefficient is age's effect \emph{beyond} what size
already explains.

\textbf{Notes.} To ask whether \emph{any} predictor matters, use the
F-statistic, not \texttt{p} individual t-tests. Which predictors to
include is Variable selection (→ Best subset selection, Forward and
backward stepwise selection).

\hypertarget{f-statistic}{%
\subsubsection{F-statistic}\label{f-statistic}}

\textbf{Definition.} Tests whether \emph{all} coefficients are zero:
\texttt{H\_0:\ β\_1\ =\ …\ =\ β\_p\ =\ 0} vs \texttt{H\_a:} at least one
is non-zero.
\texttt{F\ =\ {[}(TSS\ −\ RSS)/p{]}\ /\ {[}RSS/(n\ −\ p\ −\ 1){]}}.
Under \texttt{H\_0}, \texttt{F} is around 1; if some predictor matters,
\texttt{F\ \textgreater{}\ 1}.

\begin{equation*}
F=\frac{(\mathrm{TSS}-\mathrm{RSS})/p}{\mathrm{RSS}/(n-p-1)}
\qquad (F\approx 1 \text{ when } H_0:\beta_1=\dots=\beta_p=0 \text{ holds})
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} It compares variance the model explains against
variance it leaves behind. If the predictors do nothing, both are just
noise and the ratio hovers near 1; if they help, the numerator swells
and F climbs.

\textbf{Notes.} Why not just look at individual t-tests? With many
predictors, some will look significant by chance; the F-test gives one
honest overall verdict. → Multiple linear regression.

\hypertarget{qualitative-predictors-and-dummy-variables}{%
\subsubsection{Qualitative predictors and dummy
variables}\label{qualitative-predictors-and-dummy-variables}}

\textbf{Definition.} To use a categorical predictor (e.g.~student:
yes/no) in a regression, encode it as a \emph{dummy variable} (e.g.~1 if
student, 0 otherwise) and include it like any numeric predictor. A
\texttt{K}-level category needs \texttt{K\ −\ 1} dummies.

\textbf{Intuition.} A dummy lets the model shift the intercept for one
group versus a baseline --- ``students carry, on average, this much more
balance than non-students.'' Quantitative and qualitative predictors mix
freely in one model.

\textbf{Notes.} Different coding schemes give different coefficients but
equivalent fits; interpretation depends on the chosen baseline.
Interactions between a dummy and a numeric predictor let the
\emph{slope} differ by group, not just the intercept. → Interaction
terms.

\hypertarget{additivity-and-linearity-assumptions}{%
\subsubsection{Additivity and linearity
assumptions}\label{additivity-and-linearity-assumptions}}

\textbf{Definition.} Standard linear regression assumes (1)
\emph{additivity} --- the effect of \texttt{X\_j} on \texttt{Y} doesn't
depend on the other predictors' values; and (2) \emph{linearity} --- the
change in \texttt{Y} per one-unit change in \texttt{X\_j} is constant
regardless of \texttt{X\_j}.

\textbf{Intuition.} Additivity says predictors don't team up (no ``this
drug only works with that one''). Linearity says the effect is a
straight line, never bending. Both are convenient approximations that
reality often violates.

\textbf{Notes.} Relax additivity with Interaction terms; relax linearity
with Polynomial regression and the methods in Moving Beyond Linearity.

\hypertarget{interaction-terms}{%
\subsubsection{Interaction terms}\label{interaction-terms}}

\textbf{Definition.} A product predictor \texttt{X\_1\ ·\ X\_2} (with
coefficient \texttt{β\_3}) added to a model to capture a \emph{synergy}
/ \emph{interaction effect} --- where the effect of one predictor
depends on another. Its presence makes the effective slope on
\texttt{X\_1} a function of \texttt{X\_2}.

\textbf{Intuition.} Rewriting shows the \texttt{X\_1} slope becomes
\texttt{(β\_1\ +\ β\_3\ X\_2)} --- so changing \texttt{X\_2} changes how
strongly \texttt{X\_1} acts on \texttt{Y}. That's exactly ``these two
work together.'' Radio ad spend might boost the payoff of TV ad spend,
and vice versa.

\textbf{Notes.} \emph{Hierarchical principle:} if you include an
interaction, include both main effects too, even if their own p-values
look insignificant. Interactions apply to qualitative predictors as well
(a quant×qual interaction lets a group have its own slope). → Additivity
and linearity assumptions.

\hypertarget{potential-problems-in-linear-regression}{%
\subsubsection{Potential problems in linear
regression}\label{potential-problems-in-linear-regression}}

\textbf{Definition.} Six common ways a linear fit can go wrong, each
with a diagnostic: - \textbf{Non-linearity} --- the true relationship
isn't a straight line. Diagnose with a \emph{residual plot} (residuals
vs fitted values); a clear pattern signals trouble. Fix with
transformations or Polynomial regression. - \textbf{Correlated error
terms} --- errors that track each other (common in \emph{time series}),
which makes standard errors too small and confidence falsely tight. -
\textbf{Heteroscedasticity} --- non-constant error variance, often a
\emph{funnel shape} in the residual plot. Fix by transforming \texttt{Y}
(e.g.~\texttt{log\ Y} or \texttt{√Y}) or by \emph{weighted least
squares} (weight each point by the inverse of its variance). -
\textbf{Outliers} --- points with an unusually large residual. Spot them
with \emph{studentized residuals} (residual ÷ its estimated SE); a rule
of thumb flags values beyond about ±3. - \textbf{High-leverage points}
--- points with unusual \emph{predictor} values that yank the fitted
line. Quantify with the \emph{leverage statistic} \texttt{h\_i} (always
between \texttt{1/n} and 1); high leverage + large residual is
especially dangerous. - \textbf{Collinearity} --- two or more predictors
closely related, inflating coefficient standard errors so effects can't
be separated. Spot pairwise collinearity in the correlation matrix; spot
\emph{multicollinearity} (three-plus variables jointly related,
invisible pairwise) with the \emph{variance inflation factor} (VIF). VIF
≥ 1, and a VIF above \textasciitilde5--10 flags a problem. Fix by
dropping one variable or combining the collinear ones.

\textbf{Intuition.} The residual plot is your first stop for most of
these --- a good fit leaves residuals looking like structureless static.
Outliers hurt your error estimates; high-leverage points hurt the line
itself; collinearity doesn't bias the fit but makes it impossible to say
\emph{which} correlated predictor is responsible.

\textbf{Notes.} An outlier may signal a data error (safe to remove) or a
missing predictor (don't just delete it). VIF for \texttt{X\_j} is
\texttt{1/(1\ −\ R²)} from regressing \texttt{X\_j} on the other
predictors --- high shared variance → high VIF. Correlated errors
connect to time-series methods (→ Autoregressive models and
autocorrelation (ML)).

\hypertarget{knn-regression}{%
\subsubsection{KNN regression}\label{knn-regression}}

\textbf{Definition.} The regression version of KNN: for a prediction
point \texttt{x\_0}, find the \texttt{K} nearest training points
\texttt{N\_0} and predict the \emph{average} of their responses,
\texttt{(x\_0)\ =\ (1/K)\ Σ\_\{i\ ∈\ N\_0\}\ y\_i}.

\textbf{Intuition.} ``What did the \texttt{K} most similar cases do?
Average them.'' No functional form assumed --- the fit follows the data.
Small \texttt{K} = wiggly and flexible; large \texttt{K} = smooth.

\textbf{Notes.} Parametric (least squares) vs non-parametric (KNN)
trade-off: KNN wins when the true \texttt{f} is far from linear; least
squares wins when the assumed form is close to true, \emph{and} when
there are few observations per predictor. KNN degrades badly as
\texttt{p} grows (the \emph{curse of dimensionality} --- neighbors stop
being near) \emph{(beyond ISL ch.~3)}. → K-nearest neighbors (classifier
version).

\begin{center}\rule{0.5\linewidth}{0.5pt}\end{center}

\hypertarget{classification-isl-ch.-4}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Classification \emph{(ISL
ch.~4)}}{Classification (ISL ch.~4)}}\label{classification-isl-ch.-4}}

\hypertarget{why-not-linear-regression-for-classification}{%
\subsubsection{Why not linear regression for
classification}\label{why-not-linear-regression-for-classification}}

\textbf{Definition.} Two reasons linear regression is unsuitable for a
qualitative response: (a) it can't handle a response with more than two
classes (any numeric coding imposes a fake ordering and spacing); (b)
even with two classes it can produce probability estimates outside
\texttt{{[}0,\ 1{]}}, which are meaningless.

\textbf{Intuition.} Coding classes as 1, 2, 3 tells the model ``class 3
is three times class 1 and sits between 2 and beyond,'' which is
nonsense for unordered labels. And a straight line, extended far enough,
predicts probabilities below 0 or above 1.

\textbf{Notes.} Motivates logistic regression (squashes output into
\texttt{{[}0,\ 1{]}}) and the generative classifiers (LDA, QDA, naive
Bayes). → Logistic regression, Generative classifiers and Bayes'
theorem.

\hypertarget{logistic-regression}{%
\subsubsection{Logistic regression}\label{logistic-regression}}

\textbf{Definition.} Models the \emph{probability} of a class rather
than the class itself, using the logistic function
\texttt{p(X)\ =\ e\^{}(β\_0\ +\ β\_1\ X)\ /\ (1\ +\ e\^{}(β\_0\ +\ β\_1\ X))},
which always outputs a value in \texttt{(0,\ 1)}. Fit by maximum
likelihood.

\begin{equation*}
p(X)=\frac{e^{\beta_0+\beta_1X}}{1+e^{\beta_0+\beta_1X}}=\sigma(\beta_0+\beta_1X),
\qquad
\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} The logistic function bends any straight-line score
into an S-curve pinned between 0 and 1, so you always get a sensible
probability. Predict the class by thresholding (usually at 0.5). Despite
the name, it's a \emph{classification} method.

\textbf{Notes.} Coefficient significance is judged with a z-statistic
(\texttt{\_1\ /\ SE(\_1)}), the classification analogue of the
t-statistic. Extends to many predictors and to more than two classes. →
Odds and log-odds, Maximum likelihood, Multinomial logistic regression.

\hypertarget{odds-and-log-odds-logit}{%
\subsubsection{Odds and log-odds
(logit)}\label{odds-and-log-odds-logit}}

\textbf{Definition.} The \emph{odds} are \texttt{p(X)\ /\ (1\ −\ p(X))},
ranging from 0 to ∞. Taking the log gives the \emph{log-odds} or
\emph{logit}: \texttt{log{[}p(X)/(1\ −\ p(X)){]}\ =\ β\_0\ +\ β\_1\ X}
--- linear in \texttt{X}.

\begin{equation*}
\frac{p(X)}{1-p(X)}=e^{\beta_0+\beta_1X}
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\log\!\left(\frac{p(X)}{1-p(X)}\right)=\beta_0+\beta_1X
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} Probability lives on \texttt{{[}0,1{]}}, which is
awkward for a linear model. Odds stretch that to \texttt{{[}0,\ ∞)}, and
the log stretches it to the full number line --- so the logit is where
logistic regression is actually ``linear.'' A one-unit rise in
\texttt{X} adds \texttt{β\_1} to the log-odds (multiplies the odds by
\texttt{e\^{}(β\_1)}).

\textbf{Notes.} Odds near 0 = very unlikely; near ∞ = near-certain. This
log-odds-is-linear structure is what generalizes to multinomial logistic
regression and, more broadly, GLMs.

\hypertarget{maximum-likelihood}{%
\subsubsection{Maximum likelihood}\label{maximum-likelihood}}

\textbf{Definition.} The method used to fit logistic regression (and
GLMs): choose the coefficients that make the \emph{likelihood} --- the
probability of the observed data under the model --- as large as
possible.

\begin{equation*}
\ell(\beta_0,\beta_1)=\prod_{i:y_i=1}p(x_i)\prod_{i':y_{i'}=0}\bigl(1-p(x_{i'})\bigr),
\qquad
\hat\beta=\arg\max_\beta \log \ell(\beta)
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} Find the coefficients under which the events that
actually happened look most probable: push predicted probabilities
toward 1 for the cases that were positive and toward 0 for the cases
that were negative. Least squares is actually a special case of maximum
likelihood (for Gaussian errors).

\textbf{Notes.} Preferred over ad-hoc fitting because of its good
statistical properties. Also fits Poisson regression and the
coefficients in GLMs generally.

\hypertarget{multinomial-logistic-regression}{%
\subsubsection{Multinomial logistic
regression}\label{multinomial-logistic-regression}}

\textbf{Definition.} Extends logistic regression to
\texttt{K\ \textgreater{}\ 2} classes. Pick one class as a
\emph{baseline}, then model the log-odds of every other class against it
as linear in the predictors. The \emph{softmax} coding is an equivalent,
symmetric alternative that avoids singling out a baseline.

\textbf{Intuition.} With three-plus labels you can't use a single yes/no
probability. So you model each class's probability relative to a
reference, and the pieces are constrained to sum to 1. Softmax does the
same thing without a privileged reference class.

\textbf{Notes.} Coefficient interpretation depends on the baseline
choice, so read them with care. Key model outputs (fitted probabilities,
pairwise log-odds) are the same under either coding. Softmax reappears
as the output layer of classification neural nets. → Output layer and
loss (ML).

\hypertarget{confounding}{%
\subsubsection{Confounding}\label{confounding}}

\textbf{Definition.} When the result of a single-predictor regression
differs from --- even reverses --- the result using multiple predictors,
because predictors are correlated and one stands in for another.

\textbf{Intuition.} Alone, a predictor can look guilty just because it
travels with the real culprit. Add the real driver and the impostor's
effect shrinks or flips. The classic reminder that ``correlated with
\texttt{Y}'' and ``causes \texttt{Y} holding others fixed'' are
different questions.

\textbf{Notes.} Why multiple regression coefficients (``holding others
fixed'') can tell a very different story than simple regressions run one
predictor at a time. → Multiple linear regression, Collinearity (in
Potential problems).

\hypertarget{generative-classifiers-and-bayes-theorem}{%
\subsubsection{Generative classifiers and Bayes'
theorem}\label{generative-classifiers-and-bayes-theorem}}

\textbf{Definition.} Instead of modeling \texttt{Pr(Y\ \textbar{}\ X)}
directly (as logistic regression does), \emph{generative} classifiers
model how the predictors are distributed \emph{within each class} and
invert with Bayes' theorem:
\texttt{Pr(Y\ =\ k\ \textbar{}\ X\ =\ x)\ =\ π\_k\ f\_k(x)\ /\ Σ\_l\ π\_l\ f\_l(x)}.
Here \texttt{π\_k} is the \emph{prior} (overall fraction in class
\texttt{k}), \texttt{f\_k(x)} is the \emph{density} of \texttt{X} in
class \texttt{k}, and the result \texttt{p\_k(x)} is the
\emph{posterior} probability.

\begin{equation*}
\Pr(Y=k\mid X=x)=\frac{\pi_k f_k(x)}{\sum_{l=1}^{K}\pi_l f_l(x)}
\qquad
\pi_k=\text{prior},\; f_k(x)=\text{class-}k\text{ density}
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} Learn what each class's data typically looks like,
then for a new point ask ``which class's profile does this most
resemble, weighted by how common that class is?'' Bayes' theorem turns
``what does class \texttt{k} look like'' into ``given this look, how
likely is class \texttt{k}.''

\textbf{Notes.} \texttt{π\_k} is easy to estimate (class frequencies).
The three generative methods --- LDA, QDA, naive Bayes --- differ only
in how they estimate \texttt{f\_k(x)}. All approximate the Bayes
classifier. → Linear discriminant analysis, Quadratic discriminant
analysis, Naive Bayes.

\hypertarget{linear-discriminant-analysis-lda}{%
\subsubsection{Linear discriminant analysis
(LDA)}\label{linear-discriminant-analysis-lda}}

\textbf{Definition.} A generative classifier that separates classes by
finding the axis that best \emph{pushes the class means apart while
keeping each class tight}. Project the data onto a new axis chosen to
maximize \texttt{(μ\_1\ −\ μ\_2)²\ /\ (s\_1²\ +\ s\_2²)} --- squared
distance between class means over the total within-class scatter.
Equivalently (ISL framing) it assumes each class is Gaussian with
class-specific means but a \emph{shared covariance}, and plugs the
means, shared variance, and priors into Bayes' theorem; the discriminant
scores come out \emph{linear} in \texttt{x}.

\begin{equation*}
\delta_k(x)=x^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}\mu_k-\tfrac12\mu_k^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}\mu_k+\log\pi_k
\qquad\text{(assign to the }k\text{ with the largest }\delta_k)
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} Like PCA it builds a new axis out of the existing
features, but with a supervised goal: PCA maximizes total spread, LDA
maximizes \emph{separability}. Push the means apart (numerator) and
squeeze each class's scatter (denominator) so the projected groups
barely overlap. For \texttt{K\ \textgreater{}\ 2} groups, maximize the
spread of the class means around the overall (central) mean while
minimizing within-class scatter.

\textbf{Example.} Two mouse groups measured on two genes where neither
gene alone separates them: LDA finds the diagonal axis along which the
group means are far apart \emph{and} each group stays compact, so
projecting onto it makes them separable with a single threshold.

\textbf{Notes.} \emph{Correction to the jotted formula:} the denominator
is the \textbf{sum} of the within-group variances
\texttt{s\_1²\ +\ s\_2²} (total within-class scatter), \textbf{not}
their difference. Useful when classes are well-separated (where logistic
regression coefficients get unstable) and with small, roughly-normal
samples; extends naturally to \texttt{K\ \textgreater{}\ 2}. The
multivariate case uses a \texttt{p}-dimensional Gaussian with a
covariance matrix --- kept conceptual here. → Generative classifiers,
QDA (drops the shared-covariance assumption), Principal components
analysis (PCA) (ML). \emph{(StatQuest)}

\hypertarget{quadratic-discriminant-analysis-qda}{%
\subsubsection{Quadratic discriminant analysis
(QDA)}\label{quadratic-discriminant-analysis-qda}}

\textbf{Definition.} Like LDA, but each class gets its \emph{own}
covariance matrix. This makes the discriminant scores \emph{quadratic}
in \texttt{x}, allowing curved decision boundaries.

\textbf{Intuition.} Let each class's bell have its own shape and spread,
not just its own center. More flexible than LDA, so it can trace curved
boundaries --- at the cost of estimating many more parameters.

\textbf{Notes.} Bias--variance trade-off between the two: use LDA when
data are scarce (fewer parameters, lower variance) or the
shared-covariance assumption is reasonable; use QDA when the training
set is large or that assumption is clearly wrong. → LDA, Bias--variance
trade-off.

\hypertarget{naive-bayes}{%
\subsubsection{Naive Bayes}\label{naive-bayes}}

\textbf{Definition.} A generative classifier that assumes, \emph{within
each class, the predictors are independent} --- so the class density
factorizes:
\texttt{f\_k(x)\ =\ f\_\{k1\}(x\_1)\ ×\ …\ ×\ f\_\{kp\}(x\_p)}. Each
one-dimensional density \texttt{f\_\{kj\}} is estimated separately
(Gaussian, histogram, kernel, or class proportions for categoricals).

\begin{equation*}
f_k(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{p}f_{kj}(x_j)
\qquad\text{(conditional independence of the }p\text{ features given the class)}
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} Rather than model how predictors move together (hard
in high dimensions), pretend they don't --- estimate each predictor's
behavior per class on its own and multiply. The independence assumption
is usually false, yet the classifier often works well because you only
need the \emph{ranking} of class probabilities to be right.

\textbf{Notes.} Trades bias for variance: the independence assumption
adds bias but slashes the number of parameters, which helps when
\texttt{p} is large or \texttt{n} is small. Related to LDA/QDA: LDA is a
special case of naive Bayes with Gaussian, and naive Bayes with a
Gaussian is LDA with a diagonal covariance; naive Bayes and QDA are not
special cases of each other. → Generative classifiers.

\hypertarget{confusion-matrix-and-error-types}{%
\subsubsection{Confusion matrix and error
types}\label{confusion-matrix-and-error-types}}

\textbf{Definition.} A table cross-tabulating predicted vs actual
classes, exposing the \emph{two types of error} in binary
classification: false positives (predict yes when actually no) and false
negatives (predict no when actually yes).

\begin{equation*}
\text{TPR (recall)}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}},\quad
\text{FPR}=\frac{\mathrm{FP}}{\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{TN}},\quad
\text{precision}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}}
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} A single error rate hides \emph{which} mistakes you
make. In many settings (disease screening, default prediction) one error
type is far costlier than the other, and the confusion matrix lets you
see and trade between them.

\textbf{Notes.} Moving the classification threshold away from 0.5 trades
one error type for the other. Sweeping all thresholds produces the ROC
curve. → ROC curve.

\hypertarget{roc-curve}{%
\subsubsection{ROC curve}\label{roc-curve}}

\textbf{Definition.} A plot that displays the two classification error
types across \emph{all} possible thresholds simultaneously, tracing
true-positive rate against false-positive rate. (ROC = ``receiver
operating characteristics,'' a name inherited from communications
theory.)

\textbf{Intuition.} Instead of committing to one threshold, see the
whole menu of trade-offs at once. A curve hugging the top-left corner is
excellent; the diagonal is random guessing.

\textbf{Notes.} The \emph{area under the curve (AUC)} summarizes overall
performance in one number: it equals the probability that a random
positive case is scored higher than a random negative one, so a larger
AUC is better. Useful for comparing classifiers independent of a
specific threshold. → Confusion matrix and error types.

\hypertarget{poisson-regression}{%
\subsubsection{Poisson regression}\label{poisson-regression}}

\textbf{Definition.} A GLM for \emph{count} responses
(\texttt{Y\ ∈\ \{0,\ 1,\ 2,\ …\}}), which assumes \texttt{Y} follows a
Poisson distribution and models its mean as
\texttt{λ(X)\ =\ e\^{}(β\_0\ +\ β\_1\ X\_1\ +\ …\ +\ β\_p\ X\_p)}. Fit
by maximum likelihood.

\begin{equation*}
\Pr(Y=k)=\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{k}}{k!},
\qquad
\log\lambda(X)=\beta_0+\beta_1X_1+\dots+\beta_pX_p
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} Counts can't be negative and their spread grows with
their average --- a straight-line model ignores both. Poisson regression
uses a log link so predictions stay non-negative, and a one-unit rise in
\texttt{X\_j} \emph{multiplies} the expected count by
\texttt{e\^{}(β\_j)} (e.g.~\texttt{e\^{}(−0.08)\ ≈\ 0.923} → about 8\%
fewer).

\textbf{Intuition (mean--variance).} The Poisson assumes mean =
variance, so it naturally lets variability grow where the counts are
large --- something ordinary linear regression, with its constant
variance, can't do.

\textbf{Notes.} Advantages over linear regression on counts: never
predicts negative values, and it captures the mean--variance link. An
example GLM alongside linear and logistic regression. → Generalized
linear models.

\hypertarget{generalized-linear-models-glms}{%
\subsubsection{Generalized linear models
(GLMs)}\label{generalized-linear-models-glms}}

\textbf{Definition.} A family that unifies linear, logistic, and Poisson
regression: model a transformation (link) of the response's mean as a
linear function of the predictors, and fit by maximum likelihood.

\textbf{Intuition.} Same linear-predictor engine, different link and
response distribution: identity link + Gaussian = linear regression;
logit link + binomial = logistic regression; log link + Poisson =
Poisson regression. One framework, three familiar tools.

\textbf{Notes.} Logistic regression served as the jumping-off point for
this generalization. → Logistic regression, Poisson regression.

\hypertarget{resampling-methods-isl-ch.-5}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Resampling Methods \emph{(ISL
ch.~5)}}{Resampling Methods (ISL ch.~5)}}\label{resampling-methods-isl-ch.-5}}

\hypertarget{resampling-methods}{%
\subsubsection{Resampling methods}\label{resampling-methods}}

\textbf{Definition.} Techniques that repeatedly draw different subsets
from the training data, refit the model on each, and examine how the
fits vary --- to estimate test error or the variability of an estimate.
The two workhorses are cross-validation and the bootstrap.

\textbf{Intuition.} You usually can't get fresh data on demand, so you
\emph{simulate} having many datasets by resampling the one you have. The
spread of the refits tells you how much your model or estimate would
wobble on new data.

\textbf{Notes.} Computationally expensive (fit the same method many
times) but broadly applicable. → Validation set approach, k-fold
cross-validation, The bootstrap.

\hypertarget{model-assessment-vs-model-selection}{%
\subsubsection{Model assessment vs model
selection}\label{model-assessment-vs-model-selection}}

\textbf{Definition.} \emph{Model assessment} = estimating a chosen
model's test-set performance. \emph{Model selection} = choosing the
right level of flexibility (or the right model) in the first place.

\textbf{Intuition.} Two different jobs resampling handles: ``how good is
this model?'' vs ``which model should I use?'' Cross-validation serves
both.

\textbf{Notes.} Selection often means picking a tuning parameter (K in
KNN, λ in ridge/lasso, tree size, \texttt{d} in polynomials) by
minimizing cross-validated error. → k-fold cross-validation.

\hypertarget{validation-set-approach}{%
\subsubsection{Validation set approach}\label{validation-set-approach}}

\textbf{Definition.} Randomly split the data into a training set and a
\emph{validation (hold-out) set}; fit on the training half and estimate
test error on the validation half.

\textbf{Intuition.} The simplest honest test: keep some data hidden,
train, then grade yourself on the hidden part. Easy to implement, but
crude.

\textbf{Notes.} Two drawbacks: (1) the estimate is \emph{highly
variable} --- it swings depending on which points landed in which half;
(2) it \emph{overestimates} test error, because the model was trained on
only part of the data and methods do worse with less data.
Cross-validation fixes both. → Leave-one-out cross-validation, k-fold
cross-validation.

\hypertarget{leave-one-out-cross-validation-loocv}{%
\subsubsection{Leave-one-out cross-validation
(LOOCV)}\label{leave-one-out-cross-validation-loocv}}

\textbf{Definition.} Hold out a \emph{single} observation as the
validation set, train on the other \texttt{n\ −\ 1}, predict the
held-out point, and repeat for all \texttt{n} points; average the
\texttt{n} errors.

\textbf{Intuition.} Push the validation idea to its extreme: hold out
just one point at a time, so almost all the data trains the model every
round. This removes the ``trained on too little data'' bias of the
validation-set approach and, by averaging over every point, removes the
randomness of the split.

\textbf{Notes.} Downside: it fits the model \texttt{n} times, which is
costly for large \texttt{n} --- \emph{except} for least-squares
linear/polynomial regression, where a shortcut formula (using leverage
\texttt{h\_i}) makes LOOCV cost the same as a single fit. Each
single-point error is unbiased but very noisy on its own; the average is
what's useful. → k-fold cross-validation (cheaper alternative).

\hypertarget{k-fold-cross-validation}{%
\subsubsection{k-fold cross-validation}\label{k-fold-cross-validation}}

\textbf{Definition.} Randomly split the data into \texttt{k} roughly
equal \emph{folds}. In turn, hold out each fold as the validation set,
train on the other \texttt{k\ −\ 1}, and record the error; average the
\texttt{k} errors for the CV estimate. (LOOCV is the special case
\texttt{k\ =\ n}.)

\textbf{Intuition.} A middle ground: hold out a chunk at a time instead
of one point, so you refit only \texttt{k} times (typically 5 or 10)
instead of \texttt{n}. You still train on most of the data each round,
so bias stays low, and averaging tames the split randomness.

\textbf{Notes.} Cheaper than LOOCV and often has \emph{lower variance}
as a test-error estimate. In classification, swap MSE for the
misclassification rate. This is the standard tool for tuning-parameter
selection (λ, K, tree size). → Model assessment vs model selection.

\hypertarget{the-bootstrap}{%
\subsubsection{The bootstrap}\label{the-bootstrap}}

\textbf{Definition.} A general tool for quantifying the uncertainty of
an estimator: repeatedly draw \emph{bootstrap samples} of size
\texttt{n} from the data \emph{with replacement}, recompute the estimate
(mean, median, slope, \ldots) on each, and use the spread of those
\texttt{B} estimates as a standard error --- or their percentiles as a
confidence interval.

\textbf{Intuition.} Treat your sample as a stand-in for the population
and ``resample your resample.'' Because it draws with replacement, each
bootstrap dataset repeats some points and omits others, mimicking the
variation you'd see across genuinely new samples. The scatter of the
recomputed estimates is your uncertainty.

\textbf{Example.} From 12 measured mouse weights, draw 12 \emph{with
replacement} (some repeat, some drop out) and record the mean; repeat
10,000 times. The standard deviation of those 10,000 means estimates the
standard error, and their 2.5th--97.5th percentiles give a 95\% CI ---
no formula required.

\textbf{Notes.} Works for quantities with no tidy standard-error
formula. Sampling \emph{with} replacement is the crux --- it's what
makes each bootstrap dataset differ. Wants a reasonable sample to
resample from (a rough rule of thumb is \textasciitilde10+
observations). Bootstrap aggregation of this idea over trees is Bagging.
→ Bagging (ML), Standard error and the sampling distribution of the
mean, Confidence interval. \emph{(StatQuest)}

\hypertarget{linear-model-selection-and-regularization-isl-ch.-6}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Linear Model Selection and Regularization
\emph{(ISL
ch.~6)}}{Linear Model Selection and Regularization (ISL ch.~6)}}\label{linear-model-selection-and-regularization-isl-ch.-6}}

\hypertarget{why-move-beyond-least-squares}{%
\subsubsection{Why move beyond least
squares}\label{why-move-beyond-least-squares}}

\textbf{Definition.} Alternatives to plain least squares can improve
\emph{prediction accuracy} and \emph{interpretability}. Three families:
\emph{subset selection} (keep a subset of predictors),
\emph{shrinkage/regularization} (fit all predictors but shrink
coefficients toward zero), and \emph{dimension reduction} (project
predictors into fewer combined directions).

\textbf{Intuition.} Least squares has low bias but can have high
variance when \texttt{n} isn't much bigger than \texttt{p}
(overfitting), and when \texttt{p\ \textgreater{}\ n} it has no unique
solution at all. It also never zeroes out useless predictors, leaving
cluttered models. These methods trade a little bias for a lot less
variance and simpler models.

\textbf{Notes.} Shrinkage reduces variance; subset selection and the
lasso also improve interpretability by dropping variables. → Best subset
selection, Ridge regression, The lasso, Principal components regression.

\hypertarget{best-subset-selection}{%
\subsubsection{Best subset selection}\label{best-subset-selection}}

\textbf{Definition.} Fit a separate least-squares model for \emph{every}
possible subset of the \texttt{p} predictors (\texttt{2\^{}p} models),
pick the best model of each size by RSS/R², then choose among those
using an estimate of \emph{test} error (Cp, AIC, BIC, adjusted R², or
cross-validation).

\textbf{Intuition.} Brute force: try every combination and keep the
best. Note the two-stage logic --- RSS picks the best model
\emph{within} a size (bigger is always better on training data), but you
need a test-error estimate to compare \emph{across} sizes fairly.

\textbf{Notes.} Conceptually clean but computationally explosive
(\texttt{2\^{}p} grows fast), and a huge search space risks finding
models that look good by chance → overfitting. Stepwise methods are the
practical alternatives. → Forward and backward stepwise selection.

\hypertarget{forward-and-backward-stepwise-selection}{%
\subsubsection{Forward and backward stepwise
selection}\label{forward-and-backward-stepwise-selection}}

\textbf{Definition.} Greedy alternatives that explore far fewer models.
\emph{Forward}: start with no predictors, add the one that most improves
the fit, repeat. \emph{Backward}: start with all predictors, drop the
least useful (largest p-value), repeat. \emph{Mixed}: add like forward
but drop any variable whose value fades as others enter.

\textbf{Intuition.} Instead of all \texttt{2\^{}p} models, walk a path
one variable at a time. Much faster and usable when \texttt{p} is large.
Forward can even start when \texttt{p\ \textgreater{}\ n}; backward
needs \texttt{n\ \textgreater{}\ p} to begin.

\textbf{Notes.} Not guaranteed to find the best subset --- an early
greedy choice can lock out a better later combination. Mixed selection
mimics best-subset behavior while keeping the speed. As with best
subset, compare across sizes with Cp/AIC/BIC/adjusted R² or CV. → Cp,
AIC, BIC, and adjusted R-squared.

\hypertarget{cp-aic-bic-and-adjusted-r-squared}{%
\subsubsection{Cp, AIC, BIC, and adjusted
R-squared}\label{cp-aic-bic-and-adjusted-r-squared}}

\textbf{Definition.} Four ways to estimate \emph{test} error from
\emph{training} error by penalizing model size, so you can compare
models with different numbers of predictors. Mallow's \texttt{Cp} and
\texttt{AIC} add a \texttt{2d²}-style penalty to training RSS;
\texttt{BIC} uses a heavier \texttt{log(n)·d²} penalty; \emph{adjusted
R²} modifies R² to reward fit only when a new variable earns its keep.

\begin{equation*}
C_p=\tfrac{1}{n}\bigl(\mathrm{RSS}+2d\hat\sigma^2\bigr),\quad
\mathrm{AIC}=-2\log\ell+2d,\quad
\mathrm{BIC}=\tfrac{1}{n}\bigl(\mathrm{RSS}+d\hat\sigma^2\log n\bigr)
\end{equation*}

\begin{equation*}
\text{Adjusted } R^2=1-\frac{\mathrm{RSS}/(n-d-1)}{\mathrm{TSS}/(n-1)}
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} Training error always drops as you add predictors,
so it's a biased, over-optimistic gauge. These criteria charge a
``complexity tax'' per predictor to undo that bias. For Cp/AIC/BIC,
\emph{smaller is better}; for adjusted R², \emph{larger is better}.

\textbf{Notes.} BIC's steeper penalty favors smaller models than Cp/AIC.
All are cheaper than cross-validation but rest on more assumptions. →
Best subset selection, One-standard-error rule.

\hypertarget{one-standard-error-rule}{%
\subsubsection{One-standard-error rule}\label{one-standard-error-rule}}

\textbf{Definition.} After computing cross-validated (or penalized)
test-error estimates for each model size, pick the \emph{simplest} model
whose error is within one standard error of the best model's error.

\textbf{Intuition.} If several models are statistically tied, prefer the
simplest one. A slightly smaller, more interpretable model that's
essentially as accurate beats a bigger one whose edge is within the
noise.

\textbf{Notes.} A general principle, applicable wherever you tune
complexity by cross-validation (subset size, λ, tree size). → k-fold
cross-validation.

\hypertarget{ridge-regression}{%
\subsubsection{Ridge regression}\label{ridge-regression}}

\textbf{Definition.} Fits all \texttt{p} predictors but minimizes
\texttt{RSS\ +\ λ\ Σ\ β\_j²} (a squared, ``L2'' \emph{shrinkage
penalty}). The tuning parameter \texttt{λ\ ≥\ 0} controls how hard
coefficients are pulled toward zero; \texttt{λ\ =\ 0} recovers least
squares, \texttt{λ\ →\ ∞} drives coefficients toward (but not exactly
to) zero.

\begin{equation*}
\hat\beta^{\text{ridge}}=\arg\min_\beta\;\mathrm{RSS}+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_j^2
\qquad (\ell_2\text{ penalty; shrinks, never zeroes})
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} Add a cost for large coefficients, so the fit
prefers smaller, steadier ones. This \emph{shrinkage} trades a bit of
bias for a big drop in variance --- very helpful when predictors are
many or correlated. Because the penalty scales with coefficient size,
standardize predictors first so the penalty is fair across them.

\textbf{Notes.} Ridge keeps \emph{all} \texttt{p} predictors
(coefficients shrink but rarely hit zero), so it doesn't simplify
interpretation. Efficient: fitting for all \texttt{λ} at once costs
about as much as one least-squares fit. Choose \texttt{λ} by
cross-validation. → The lasso (which does drop variables), Ridge vs
lasso.

\hypertarget{the-lasso}{%
\subsubsection{The lasso}\label{the-lasso}}

\textbf{Definition.} Like ridge, but with an absolute-value (``L1'')
penalty: minimize \texttt{RSS\ +\ λ\ Σ\ \textbar{}β\_j\textbar{}}. This
penalty can force some coefficients \emph{exactly} to zero, so the lasso
performs \emph{variable selection} and yields \emph{sparse} models.

\begin{equation*}
\hat\beta^{\text{lasso}}=\arg\min_\beta\;\mathrm{RSS}+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{p}|\beta_j|
\qquad (\ell_1\text{ penalty; drives coefficients exactly to }0)
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} The corner-shaped L1 penalty makes it optimal to
snap small coefficients all the way to zero rather than merely shrink
them. So the lasso both regularizes \emph{and} selects --- you get a
smaller, more interpretable model automatically. Mechanically it shaves
a constant \texttt{λ/2} off each coefficient (soft-thresholding),
zeroing any smaller than that.

\textbf{Notes.} Big interpretability win over ridge (a subset of
predictors, not all \texttt{p}). Choose \texttt{λ} by cross-validation.
→ Ridge vs lasso.

\hypertarget{ridge-vs-lasso}{%
\subsubsection{Ridge vs lasso}\label{ridge-vs-lasso}}

\textbf{Definition.} Ridge shrinks all coefficients proportionally and
keeps every predictor; the lasso can zero coefficients out and select a
subset. Neither dominates.

\textbf{Intuition.} Lasso tends to win when only a \emph{few} predictors
truly matter and the rest are near-zero --- it cleanly discards the
deadwood. Ridge tends to win when the response depends on \emph{many}
predictors of similar size --- it keeps them all, gently. Since you
never know which regime you're in, let cross-validation decide.

\textbf{Notes.} Both reduce variance via shrinkage; the difference is
sparsity. → Ridge regression, The lasso.

\hypertarget{principal-components-regression-pcr}{%
\subsubsection{Principal components regression
(PCR)}\label{principal-components-regression-pcr}}

\textbf{Definition.} A dimension-reduction method: replace the
\texttt{p} predictors with \texttt{M\ \textless{}\ p} \emph{principal
components} (linear combinations capturing the most variance in the
predictors), then regress \texttt{Y} on those \texttt{M} components by
least squares.

\textbf{Intuition.} Instead of using every correlated predictor, distill
them into a few directions that capture most of their variation, and
regress on those. Fewer, uncorrelated inputs → lower variance. The first
component is the single direction the data spread out along most; each
next one is the biggest remaining spread, perpendicular to the earlier
ones.

\textbf{Notes.} The components are chosen \emph{unsupervised} --- using
only \texttt{X}, ignoring \texttt{Y} --- so there's no guarantee the
highest-variance directions are the best \emph{predictors}. Partial
least squares fixes the ``ignores \texttt{Y}'' gap. The
component-finding procedure itself is Principal components analysis
(PCA), also an unsupervised tool in its own right. → Partial least
squares, Principal components analysis (PCA) (ML), High-dimensional
data.

\hypertarget{partial-least-squares-pls}{%
\subsubsection{Partial least squares
(PLS)}\label{partial-least-squares-pls}}

\textbf{Definition.} A \emph{supervised} dimension-reduction method:
like PCR, but the derived directions are chosen using \texttt{Y} too,
weighting each predictor by its correlation with the response.

\textbf{Intuition.} Same ``combine predictors into a few directions''
idea as PCR, but aimed --- build directions that not only summarize the
predictors but also relate to the response. It places the most weight on
the predictors most correlated with \texttt{Y}.

\textbf{Notes.} Addresses PCR's blind spot (high-variance ≠
high-predictive directions), though in practice the gain over PCR is
often modest. → Principal components regression.

\hypertarget{high-dimensional-data}{%
\subsubsection{High-dimensional data}\label{high-dimensional-data}}

\textbf{Definition.} The setting where the number of features \texttt{p}
is large relative to (or exceeds) the number of observations \texttt{n}
--- increasingly common in genomics, finance, marketing.

\textbf{Intuition.} When \texttt{p\ ≥\ n}, least squares can fit the
training data \emph{perfectly} regardless of whether the predictors mean
anything --- which is disastrous overfitting. Training R² marches to 1
and training MSE to 0 as you add features, while \emph{test} error
explodes, because each added predictor inflates variance.

\textbf{Notes.} \emph{Emphasized:} adding features truly related to
\texttt{Y} helps; adding noise features hurts, raising test error.
Methods that regularize or reduce dimension (forward selection, ridge,
lasso, PCR) are essential here. A face of the curse of dimensionality. →
Why move beyond least squares, The lasso.

\hypertarget{moving-beyond-linearity-isl-ch.-7}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Moving Beyond Linearity \emph{(ISL
ch.~7)}}{Moving Beyond Linearity (ISL ch.~7)}}\label{moving-beyond-linearity-isl-ch.-7}}

\hypertarget{polynomial-regression}{%
\subsubsection{Polynomial regression}\label{polynomial-regression}}

\textbf{Definition.} Extends linear regression by adding powers of a
predictor as extra terms --- e.g.~cubic regression uses \texttt{X},
\texttt{X²}, \texttt{X³}. Still a linear model (in the coefficients),
just with transformed predictors.

\begin{equation*}
y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i+\beta_2x_i^2+\dots+\beta_dx_i^d+\varepsilon_i
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} Let the fit curve by feeding it \texttt{X} and its
powers. A cheap first step toward non-linearity.

\textbf{Notes.} Degrees above 3--4 are rarely used --- high-degree
polynomials wiggle wildly, especially near the edges of the data. They
impose one \emph{global} shape on the whole range, which motivates the
piecewise methods below. → Step functions, Regression splines.

\hypertarget{step-functions}{%
\subsubsection{Step functions}\label{step-functions}}

\textbf{Definition.} Cut the range of \texttt{X} into \texttt{K} bins
and fit a separate \emph{constant} in each --- a piecewise-constant fit.
This turns a continuous predictor into an ordered categorical one.

\textbf{Intuition.} Rather than one global curve, chop \texttt{X} into
regions and predict a flat level in each, like a staircase. Avoids
imposing a single global structure.

\textbf{Notes.} Weakness: unless there are natural breakpoints, the flat
steps can miss the action between cut points. A special case of the
basis-function approach. → Basis functions.

\hypertarget{basis-functions}{%
\subsubsection{Basis functions}\label{basis-functions}}

\textbf{Definition.} A general framework: fit
\texttt{y\ =\ β\_0\ +\ β\_1\ b\_1(X)\ +\ …\ +\ β\_K\ b\_K(X)}, where the
\texttt{b\_j} are \emph{fixed, known} transformations of \texttt{X}.
Polynomials and step functions are both special cases (with
\texttt{b\_j(X)\ =\ X\^{}j} or bin indicators); other choices include
splines, wavelets, and Fourier series.

\begin{equation*}
y_i=\beta_0+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_k\,b_k(x_i)+\varepsilon_i
\qquad\text{(still linear in }\beta\text{, so ordinary least squares applies)}
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} Pick a menu of building-block functions, transform
\texttt{X} through them, then fit an ordinary linear model on the
transformed inputs. All the machinery of linear regression (standard
errors, tests) still applies.

\textbf{Notes.} The unifying idea behind polynomial regression, step
functions, and Regression splines.

\hypertarget{regression-splines}{%
\subsubsection{Regression splines}\label{regression-splines}}

\textbf{Definition.} Divide the range of \texttt{X} at \emph{knots} into
regions and fit a low-degree polynomial (commonly cubic) within each,
constrained so the pieces join \emph{smoothly} at the knots (continuous
value and first/second derivatives). More knots → more flexibility.

\textbf{Intuition.} Instead of one high-degree polynomial across the
whole range, stitch together many low-degree ones and force smooth seams
--- flexible where you need it without the wild global swings of
high-degree polynomials. Each smoothness constraint at a knot ``costs''
one degree of freedom.

\textbf{Notes.} Splines add flexibility by adding \emph{knots} while
keeping the degree fixed --- usually giving better results than raising
a polynomial's degree. Where to place knots: often uniformly; more knots
where the function changes fast. → Natural splines, Smoothing splines,
Basis functions.

\hypertarget{natural-splines}{%
\subsubsection{Natural splines}\label{natural-splines}}

\textbf{Definition.} A regression spline with extra \emph{boundary
constraints}: the fit is forced to be linear beyond the outermost knots.

\textbf{Intuition.} Regular splines behave erratically at the edges
(little data, high variance). Pinning the tails to straight lines tames
that boundary wildness, giving more sensible extrapolation.

\textbf{Notes.} Typically produces a more reasonable fit near the
boundaries than an unconstrained spline or a high-degree polynomial. →
Regression splines.

\hypertarget{smoothing-splines}{%
\subsubsection{Smoothing splines}\label{smoothing-splines}}

\textbf{Definition.} Fit a smooth curve \texttt{g} by minimizing
\texttt{Σ(y\_i\ −\ g(x\_i))²\ +\ λ\ ∫\ g\textquotesingle{}\textquotesingle{}(t)²\ dt}
--- a fit-the-data term plus a \emph{roughness penalty} on the curve's
wiggliness, tuned by \texttt{λ}.

\textbf{Intuition.} You could make training error zero by threading a
curve through every point, but that curve would be absurdly wiggly. The
penalty on the second derivative (a measure of roughness) discourages
that. \texttt{λ} sets the trade-off: \texttt{λ\ =\ 0} interpolates
everything; \texttt{λ\ →\ ∞} forces a straight line.

\textbf{Notes.} No need to choose knots --- there's effectively one at
every observation; instead you choose \texttt{λ}, naturally by
cross-validation (LOOCV is especially cheap here). The second derivative
measures roughness because it tracks how fast the slope changes. →
Regression splines, k-fold cross-validation.

\hypertarget{local-regression}{%
\subsubsection{Local regression}\label{local-regression}}

\textbf{Definition.} Estimate the fit at a target point \texttt{x\_0}
using only nearby training points, weighting closer points more, and
fitting a small (often linear) weighted least-squares model there.
Repeat for each target point. The key tuning knob is the \emph{span}
\texttt{s} --- the fraction of points used.

\textbf{Intuition.} Slide a weighted window along \texttt{X}; at each
spot fit a tiny local line using mostly the neighbors. Small span →
local and wiggly; large span → smooth and global. Like KNN, it's
``memory-based'': it needs all the training data at prediction time.

\textbf{Example.} Fit \textbf{LOESS} to a noisy expression-vs-time
scatter with span 0.3: at each time point it fits a weighted line to the
\textasciitilde30\% nearest points, tracing a smooth trend curve that
follows local bumps without the wild edge swings of a high-degree
polynomial.

\textbf{Notes.} Neighborhoods overlap smoothly, unlike the hard bins of
step functions. Extends to \emph{varying-coefficient} models. Commonly
called \textbf{LOWESS}/\textbf{LOESS} (locally weighted scatterplot
smoothing) --- the smooth curve drawn through a noisy scatter. →
K-nearest neighbors, Step functions. \emph{(StatQuest)}

\hypertarget{generalized-additive-models-gams}{%
\subsubsection{Generalized additive models
(GAMs)}\label{generalized-additive-models-gams}}

\textbf{Definition.} Extend multiple linear regression by replacing each
linear term \texttt{β\_j\ x\_j} with a smooth non-linear function
\texttt{f\_j(x\_j)}, then \emph{adding} the pieces:
\texttt{y\ =\ β\_0\ +\ f\_1(x\_1)\ +\ …\ +\ f\_p(x\_p)\ +\ ε}. Works for
quantitative and qualitative responses.

\begin{equation*}
y_i=\beta_0+\sum_{j=1}^{p}f_j(x_{ij})+\varepsilon_i
\qquad\text{(one smooth function per predictor, added together)}
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} Give every predictor its own flexible curve, but
keep them \emph{additive} --- the total is still a sum of per-predictor
effects. This automatically models non-linearities linear regression
would miss, without hand-picking transformations, while staying
readable: you can see each predictor's individual effect holding others
fixed.

\textbf{Notes.} Each \texttt{f\_j}'s flexibility is summarized by its
degrees of freedom. Main limitation: pure additivity misses interactions
among predictors --- though you can add interaction terms or
low-dimensional joint functions \texttt{f\_\{jk\}(x\_j,\ x\_k)} by hand.
A sweet spot between interpretable linear models and fully flexible
black boxes. → Flexibility vs interpretability, Smoothing splines.

\hypertarget{survival-analysis-and-censored-data-isl-ch.-11}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Survival Analysis and Censored Data
\emph{(ISL
ch.~11)}}{Survival Analysis and Censored Data (ISL ch.~11)}}\label{survival-analysis-and-censored-data-isl-ch.-11}}

\hypertarget{survival-analysis-and-censored-data}{%
\subsubsection{Survival analysis and censored
data}\label{survival-analysis-and-censored-data}}

\textbf{Definition.} Methods for a response that is the \emph{time until
an event} (death, failure, churn). Data are \emph{censored} when the
event hasn't occurred by the end of observation --- you know the
survival time exceeds some value but not its exact value.

\textbf{Intuition.} A patient still alive at a study's end gives real
information (``survived at least 5 years'') that you shouldn't discard,
yet can't treat as an ordinary observed time either. Survival analysis
is built to use complete and censored observations together.

\textbf{Notes.} Ordinary regression can't handle censoring directly. →
Survival function, Hazard function, Cox proportional hazards model.

\hypertarget{survival-function}{%
\subsubsection{Survival function}\label{survival-function}}

\textbf{Definition.} \texttt{S(t)\ =\ Pr(T\ \textgreater{}\ t)} --- the
probability of surviving (not yet experiencing the event) past time
\texttt{t}. A decreasing function of \texttt{t}.

\begin{equation*}
S(t)=\Pr(T>t)=1-F(t)
\qquad\text{non-increasing, }S(0)=1
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} ``What fraction are still event-free at time
\texttt{t}?'' Starts at 1 and falls toward 0.

\textbf{Notes.} Estimated from censored data by the Kaplan-Meier curve.
→ Kaplan-Meier estimator, Hazard function.

\hypertarget{kaplan-meier-estimator}{%
\subsubsection{Kaplan-Meier estimator}\label{kaplan-meier-estimator}}

\textbf{Definition.} A non-parametric estimate of the survival function,
formed as a running product of ``survived this step'' probabilities
across the observed event times, giving a step-like curve.

\begin{equation*}
\hat S(t)=\prod_{j:\,t_j\le t}\left(1-\frac{d_j}{r_j}\right)
\qquad d_j=\text{deaths at }t_j,\; r_j=\text{at risk just before }t_j
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} At each time an event occurs, multiply in the
fraction who made it through that step; censored subjects drop out
without counting as events. The curve steps down at each event time.

\textbf{Notes.} The standard descriptive tool for survival data. →
Survival function, Log-rank test.

\hypertarget{log-rank-test}{%
\subsubsection{Log-rank test}\label{log-rank-test}}

\textbf{Definition.} A hypothesis test comparing the survival curves of
two (or more) groups, built as a standardized statistic
\texttt{W\ =\ (observed\ −\ expected)\ /\ sqrt(variance)} accumulated
over event times.

\textbf{Intuition.} At each event time, compare how many events actually
occurred in a group to how many you'd expect if the groups shared one
survival curve; sum the discrepancies. A large statistic means the
curves differ.

\textbf{Notes.} The survival analogue of a two-sample test. →
Kaplan-Meier estimator.

\hypertarget{hazard-function}{%
\subsubsection{Hazard function}\label{hazard-function}}

\textbf{Definition.} The \emph{hazard rate} \texttt{h(t)} is the
instantaneous event rate at time \texttt{t} given survival up to
\texttt{t}: \texttt{h(t)\ =\ f(t)\ /\ S(t)}, where \texttt{f(t)} is the
event-time density and \texttt{S(t)} the survival function.

\begin{equation*}
h(t)=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{\Pr(t\le T<t+\Delta t\mid T\ge t)}{\Delta t}
=\frac{f(t)}{S(t)}
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} ``Given you've made it to time \texttt{t}, how
likely is the event right now?'' The survival function \texttt{S(t)},
density \texttt{f(t)}, and hazard \texttt{h(t)} are three equivalent
ways to describe the same event-time distribution.

\textbf{Notes.} Central to the Cox model. → Cox proportional hazards
model, Survival function.

\hypertarget{cox-proportional-hazards-model}{%
\subsubsection{Cox proportional hazards
model}\label{cox-proportional-hazards-model}}

\textbf{Definition.} A regression for survival data:
\texttt{h(t\ \textbar{}\ x)\ =\ h\_0(t)\ ·\ exp(Σ\_j\ x\_j\ β\_j)},
where \texttt{h\_0(t)} is an unspecified \emph{baseline hazard} (the
hazard when all predictors are zero). It estimates the coefficients
\texttt{β} \emph{without} specifying the form of \texttt{h\_0(t)}.

\begin{equation*}
h(t\mid x)=h_0(t)\,\exp\!\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_jx_j\Bigr)
\qquad h_0(t)\text{ left unspecified (semi-parametric)}
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} Predictors don't reshape the baseline risk over time
--- they only scale it up or down by a constant factor
\texttt{exp(Σ\ x\_j\ β\_j)} (hence ``proportional hazards''). The
model's trick is learning how predictors matter while leaving the
baseline hazard completely free.

\textbf{Notes.} The workhorse regression method for censored
time-to-event data. → Hazard function.

\hypertarget{multiple-testing-isl-ch.-13}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Multiple Testing \emph{(ISL
ch.~13)}}{Multiple Testing (ISL ch.~13)}}\label{multiple-testing-isl-ch.-13}}

\hypertarget{type-i-and-type-ii-errors}{%
\subsubsection{Type I and Type II
errors}\label{type-i-and-type-ii-errors}}

\textbf{Definition.} In a hypothesis test, a \emph{Type I error} (false
positive) is rejecting a true null hypothesis; a \emph{Type II error}
(false negative) is failing to reject a false null. The Type I error
rate is the probability of a false positive.

\textbf{Intuition.} Type I = crying wolf (declaring an effect that isn't
there); Type II = missing a real effect. Lowering one generally raises
the other. The four-step test (state hypotheses, compute a statistic,
get a p-value, decide) controls Type I error at a chosen level
\texttt{α}.

\textbf{Notes.} They trade off much like bias and variance. → Hypothesis
test, Family-wise error rate.

\hypertarget{family-wise-error-rate-fwer}{%
\subsubsection{Family-wise error rate
(FWER)}\label{family-wise-error-rate-fwer}}

\textbf{Definition.} When testing \texttt{m} hypotheses at once, the
FWER is the probability of making \emph{at least one} Type I error:
\texttt{Pr(V\ ≥\ 1)}, where \texttt{V} counts false positives.

\textbf{Intuition.} Run enough tests at the usual 5\% each and false
positives become nearly guaranteed --- flip 100 fair coins and some will
``look'' biased. FWER control keeps the chance of \emph{any} false
discovery low across the whole family.

\textbf{Notes.} Controlling FWER is strict; with large \texttt{m} it
gets too conservative, motivating FDR. → Bonferroni correction, Holm's
method, False discovery rate.

\hypertarget{bonferroni-correction}{%
\subsubsection{Bonferroni correction}\label{bonferroni-correction}}

\textbf{Definition.} A simple FWER-control rule: reject a hypothesis
only if its p-value is below \texttt{α/m} (the target level divided by
the number of tests).

\begin{equation*}
\text{reject }H_{0j}\text{ when } p_j\le \frac{\alpha}{m}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathrm{FWER}\le\alpha
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} Split your error budget evenly across all tests so
their combined false-positive chance stays under \texttt{α}. Dead simple
and assumption-free, but conservative --- it rejects few hypotheses,
risking many Type II errors.

\textbf{Notes.} Makes no assumptions about the tests' dependence. →
Family-wise error rate, Holm's method.

\hypertarget{holms-method}{%
\subsubsection{Holm's method}\label{holms-method}}

\textbf{Definition.} A step-down FWER-control procedure: sort the
p-values ascending and compare each to a threshold that loosens as you
proceed, rejecting until one fails.

\textbf{Intuition.} Like Bonferroni but less harsh --- it controls FWER
just as validly while rejecting more hypotheses (fewer Type II errors,
more power), at no cost in assumptions.

\textbf{Notes.} Generally preferred over plain Bonferroni. → Bonferroni
correction, Family-wise error rate.

\hypertarget{false-discovery-rate-fdr}{%
\subsubsection{False discovery rate
(FDR)}\label{false-discovery-rate-fdr}}

\textbf{Definition.} The expected \emph{fraction} of false positives
among all rejected hypotheses: \texttt{FDR\ =\ E{[}V/R{]}}, where
\texttt{V} is false positives and \texttt{R} total rejections. The
realized ratio \texttt{V/R} is the \emph{false discovery proportion}
(FDP).

\textbf{Intuition.} With thousands of tests, demanding zero false
positives (FWER) is hopeless. Instead, tolerate some --- just keep the
\emph{proportion} of your ``discoveries'' that are false below a level
\texttt{q} (say 10\%). A far more practical target at scale.

\textbf{Notes.} Controlled by the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. →
Family-wise error rate, Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.

\hypertarget{benjamini-hochberg-procedure}{%
\subsubsection{Benjamini-Hochberg
procedure}\label{benjamini-hochberg-procedure}}

\textbf{Definition.} A procedure that controls the FDR at a chosen level
\texttt{q}: sort the p-values ascending, find the largest \texttt{j}
with \texttt{p\_(j)\ \textless{}\ q·j/m}, and reject all hypotheses up
to that one.

\begin{equation*}
\text{find } L=\max\Bigl\{j: p_{(j)}\le \frac{j}{m}q\Bigr\},\ \text{reject } H_{0(1)},\dots,H_{0(L)}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathrm{FDR}\le q
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} A ranked cutoff that lets more discoveries through
than FWER methods while guaranteeing the false-discovery \emph{fraction}
stays ≤ \texttt{q}. The standard tool for large-scale testing (genomics,
screening).

\textbf{Notes.} → False discovery rate, Family-wise error rate.

\hypertarget{statistical-inference-and-distributions-statquest}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Statistical Inference and Distributions
\emph{(StatQuest)}}{Statistical Inference and Distributions (StatQuest)}}\label{statistical-inference-and-distributions-statquest}}

\hypertarget{normal-distribution}{%
\subsubsection{Normal distribution}\label{normal-distribution}}

\textbf{Definition.} The bell-shaped continuous distribution
\texttt{N(μ,\ σ²)} with density
\texttt{f(x)\ =\ (1/(σ·√(2π)))·e\^{}(−(x−μ)²/(2σ²))}, symmetric about
its mean \texttt{μ}, with spread set by the standard deviation
\texttt{σ}.

\begin{equation*}
f(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
\qquad X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} Many measurements (heights, weights, measurement
noise) pile up around a central value and thin out symmetrically.
\texttt{μ} locates the peak; \texttt{σ} sets the width --- larger
\texttt{σ}, flatter and wider.

\textbf{Example (68--95--99.7 rule).} For any \texttt{N(μ,\ σ)}: about
\textbf{68\%} of values fall within \texttt{μ\ ±\ 1σ}, \textbf{95\%}
within \texttt{μ\ ±\ 2σ}, and \textbf{99.7\%} within \texttt{μ\ ±\ 3σ}.
If mouse weights are \texttt{N(20,\ 2²)} g, \textasciitilde95\% of mice
weigh between 16 and 24 g.

\textbf{Notes.} The reference distribution behind z-scores, standard
errors, confidence intervals, t-tests, and LDA's class model. → Z-scores
(standardization), Standard error and the sampling distribution of the
mean, Probability distributions.

\hypertarget{probability-distributions}{%
\subsubsection{Probability
distributions}\label{probability-distributions}}

\textbf{Definition.} A function giving the probability (for discrete
variables) or probability \emph{density} (for continuous ones) of each
possible value; the area under it over a range is the probability of
landing in that range, and the total area is 1.

\textbf{Intuition.} If you know the distribution's shape, you can
estimate how likely any value or bin is \emph{without} collecting every
data point --- the distribution stands in for the whole population.

\textbf{Example.} Knowing adult heights are ≈ \texttt{N(170,\ 7²)} cm
lets you say a random person is \textasciitilde95\% likely to be
156--184 cm, and that someone 200 cm tall is very rare --- all read off
the distribution, no new data.

\textbf{Notes.} Common ones: normal (continuous, symmetric), binomial
(counts of successes in \texttt{n} trials), Poisson (counts per
interval). → Normal distribution, Poisson regression.

\hypertarget{standard-error-and-the-sampling-distribution-of-the-mean}{%
\subsubsection{Standard error and the sampling distribution of the
mean}\label{standard-error-and-the-sampling-distribution-of-the-mean}}

\textbf{Definition.} Take many samples, compute each one's mean, and
those means form a distribution --- the \emph{sampling distribution of
the mean}. Its standard deviation is the \emph{standard error of the
mean}: \texttt{SE\ =\ σ/√n} (estimated by \texttt{s/√n}).

\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{SE}(\bar x)=\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}
\qquad\text{(shrinks as }\sqrt{n}\text{: 4x the data halves the error)}
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} A standard deviation describes the spread of the
\emph{data}; a standard error describes the spread of a \emph{statistic}
(here the mean) across repeated samples --- i.e.~how precisely you've
pinned the mean down. Bigger \texttt{n} → smaller SE, because averaging
more points steadies the estimate (the \texttt{√n}).

\textbf{Example.} 100 mouse weights with \texttt{s\ =\ 4\ g} give
\texttt{SE\ =\ 4/√100\ =\ 0.4\ g}. So the sample mean is precise to
about ±0.8 g (2·SE) at 95\%, even though individual mice vary by ±8 g
(2·s).

\textbf{Notes.} Don't confuse the two: report the standard deviation to
describe variability in the data, the standard error to describe
uncertainty in the mean. Bootstrapping estimates the same SE without the
formula. → Error bars (std dev, standard error, confidence interval),
Confidence interval, The bootstrap, Standard error of a coefficient.

\hypertarget{error-bars-std-dev-standard-error-confidence-interval}{%
\subsubsection{Error bars (std dev, standard error, confidence
interval)}\label{error-bars-std-dev-standard-error-confidence-interval}}

\textbf{Definition.} Three different things a ± bar on a plot can
represent: \emph{standard deviation} (spread of the data),
\emph{standard error} (\texttt{σ/√n}, uncertainty of the mean), or a
\emph{confidence interval} (usually 95\%, ≈ mean ± 2·SE).

\textbf{Intuition.} They answer different questions --- how variable the
data are, vs how precise the mean is, vs the plausible range for the
true mean --- and they get smaller in that order (\texttt{SE\ =\ SD/√n};
a 95\% CI ≈ 2·SE). Always state which one a bar shows, or it's
meaningless.

\textbf{Example.} For 100 mice with \texttt{SD\ =\ 4\ g}:
\texttt{SE\ =\ 0.4\ g} and \texttt{95\%\ CI\ ≈\ ±0.8\ g}. A ±4 g bar
(SD) looks alarming, but the ±0.8 g bar (CI) shows the \emph{mean} is
actually well-pinned.

\textbf{Notes.} SD is fixed by the data; SE and CI tighten as \texttt{n}
grows. → Standard error and the sampling distribution of the mean,
Confidence interval.

\hypertarget{z-scores-standardization}{%
\subsubsection{Z-scores
(standardization)}\label{z-scores-standardization}}

\textbf{Definition.} Rescale a value by how many standard deviations it
sits from the mean: \texttt{z\ =\ (x\ −\ μ)/σ}. Applied to every value,
it produces data with mean 0 and standard deviation 1.

\begin{equation*}
z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}
\qquad\text{(units of standard deviations from the mean)}
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} Puts different features on one comparable scale and
makes ``how unusual is this?'' explicit --- \texttt{z\ =\ 2} means two
SDs above average (\textasciitilde top 2.5\% under a normal).

\textbf{Example (heatmaps).} To build a gene-expression heatmap: for
each gene, subtract its mean and divide by its SD (z-score its row) so
highly- and lowly-expressed genes become comparable; then cluster the
rows (and columns) by similarity and colour cells by z-score. Clustering
needs a \emph{distance} --- \emph{Euclidean} \texttt{√Σ(a\_i\ −\ b\_i)²}
or \emph{Manhattan} \texttt{Σ\textbar{}a\_i\ −\ b\_i\textbar{}} --- plus
a \emph{linkage} rule (merge on the nearest, farthest, or average pair)
to decide which clusters join.

\textbf{Notes.} Standardization is the same operation as scikit-learn's
\texttt{StandardScaler}. → Feature scaling (ML), Hierarchical clustering
(ML), Normal distribution.

\hypertarget{t-tests-one-sample-paired-two-sample}{%
\subsubsection{t-tests (one-sample, paired,
two-sample)}\label{t-tests-one-sample-paired-two-sample}}

\textbf{Definition.} Tests about means using
\texttt{t\ =\ (difference\ in\ means)\ /\ (standard\ error\ of\ that\ difference)},
compared to a t-distribution. Flavours: \emph{one-sample} (a mean vs a
fixed value), \emph{paired} (same subjects measured twice ---
e.g.~before/after), and \emph{two-sample / unpaired} (two independent
groups). The unpaired test has \emph{equal-variance} (pooled) and
\emph{unequal-variance} (Welch) variants.

\begin{equation*}
t_{\text{one-sample}}=\frac{\bar x-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}},
\qquad
t_{\text{two-sample}}=\frac{\bar x_1-\bar x_2}{\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1+s_2^2/n_2}}
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Intuition.} \texttt{t} asks ``how many standard errors apart are
the means?'' Large \texttt{\textbar{}t\textbar{}} → the gap is unlikely
to be noise → small p-value. Pairing removes between-subject variability
(each subject is its own control), so a paired test is more powerful
when it applies.

\textbf{Example.} Blood pressure before vs after a drug on the
\emph{same} 20 patients → a \textbf{paired} t-test on the 20
differences. Drug vs placebo on two \emph{different} groups →
\textbf{unpaired}; if you can't assume equal variances, use
\textbf{Welch's} (unequal-variance) as the safe default.

\textbf{Notes.} Default to a \textbf{two-tailed} test unless you have a
strict, pre-specified directional hypothesis. Comparing 3+ groups → use
ANOVA, not many t-tests (which inflate false positives). → One-tailed vs
two-tailed tests, ANOVA (analysis of variance), Hypothesis test,
t-statistic, and p-value.

\hypertarget{one-tailed-vs-two-tailed-tests}{%
\subsubsection{One-tailed vs two-tailed
tests}\label{one-tailed-vs-two-tailed-tests}}

\textbf{Definition.} A \emph{two-tailed} test asks ``is there a
difference in \textbf{either} direction?''
(\texttt{H\_a:\ μ\_1\ ≠\ μ\_2}); a \emph{one-tailed} test asks about a
\textbf{single} direction (\texttt{H\_a:\ μ\_1\ \textgreater{}\ μ\_2}),
putting the whole rejection region in one tail.

\textbf{Intuition.} One-tailed has more power to detect an effect in the
direction you specified, but it is \textbf{blind} to an effect the other
way --- so it can claim ``better'' but can never notice ``worse.''
Two-tailed is the honest default when either direction matters.

\textbf{Example.} Testing a new drug two-tailed catches both ``helps''
and ``harms''; a one-tailed \texttt{drug\ \textgreater{}\ placebo} test
would miss a drug that's actively harmful. Only choose one-tailed when
the opposite direction is genuinely irrelevant and decided in advance.

\textbf{Notes.} A one-tailed p-value is half the two-tailed one (for
symmetric distributions) --- which is exactly why switching to it
\emph{after} seeing the data is p-hacking. → t-tests (one-sample,
paired, two-sample), Hypothesis test, t-statistic, and p-value.

\hypertarget{anova-analysis-of-variance}{%
\subsubsection{ANOVA (analysis of
variance)}\label{anova-analysis-of-variance}}

\textbf{Definition.} Tests whether \textbf{3+ group means} are all equal
(\texttt{H\_0:\ μ\_1\ =\ μ\_2\ =\ …\ =\ μ\_k}) with an F-statistic:
\texttt{F\ =\ (variance\ between\ groups)\ /\ (variance\ within\ groups)}.
\texttt{F} near 1 → groups look alike; \texttt{F} well above 1 → at
least one mean differs.

\textbf{Intuition.} Running many pairwise t-tests inflates false
positives; ANOVA gives one overall verdict. If the spread \emph{between}
group means dwarfs the spread \emph{within} groups, the grouping is
explaining real structure.

\textbf{Example.} Mean yield across 4 fertilizers gives
\texttt{F\ =\ 6.2}, \texttt{p\ =\ 0.002} → they're not all equal. A
significant ANOVA says ``some group differs'' but not which, so follow
up with corrected pairwise tests (Tukey/Bonferroni) to locate the
differences.

\textbf{Notes.} Same variance-ratio idea as the regression F-statistic.
→ F-statistic, Multiple Testing, Bonferroni correction.

\hypertarget{fishers-exact-test}{%
\subsubsection{Fisher's exact test}\label{fishers-exact-test}}

\textbf{Definition.} An exact test of association in a 2×2 count table.
Using the hypergeometric distribution it computes the probability of the
observed table (with row/column totals held fixed), and the p-value is
the \textbf{sum of the probabilities of all tables as rare or rarer}
than the one observed.

\textbf{Intuition.} It enumerates every table consistent with the
margins and adds up the probability sitting on outcomes at least as
extreme as yours --- no large-sample approximation, so it's the right
tool for small counts where the chi-squared test is unreliable.

\textbf{Example.} 3/10 treated vs 8/10 controls fell ill.~Fisher's test
takes the hypergeometric probability of that split plus every
more-extreme split; summing gives, say, \texttt{p\ =\ 0.07} --- not
quite significant. (Only the counts matter, not the order within a
group.)

\textbf{Notes.} Exact but expensive for large tables; chi-squared
approximates it once counts are big. → Type I and Type II errors,
Multiple Testing.

\hypertarget{statistical-power-and-power-analysis}{%
\subsubsection{Statistical power and power
analysis}\label{statistical-power-and-power-analysis}}

\textbf{Definition.} \emph{Power} is the probability a test
\textbf{detects a real effect} when one truly exists:
\texttt{power\ =\ 1\ −\ β}, where \texttt{β} is the Type II
(false-negative) rate. \emph{Power analysis} solves for the sample size
needed to reach a target power (commonly 0.8) before the study runs.

\textbf{Intuition.} Underpowered studies miss true effects and give
irreproducible results. Power rises with \textbf{effect size} (bigger
differences are easier to see), \textbf{sample size} \texttt{n},
\textbf{lower noise} (variance), and a \textbf{less strict} \texttt{α}.
Compute it \emph{first} so you collect enough data.

\textbf{Example.} To detect a 5-unit difference with \texttt{SD\ =\ 10}
at \texttt{α\ =\ 0.05} and 80\% power, a calculation might call for
\textasciitilde64 subjects per group; halving the expected effect
roughly \emph{quadruples} the \texttt{n} needed.

\textbf{Notes.} The four knobs --- effect size, variance, \texttt{n},
\texttt{α} --- trade off; fix any three to solve for the fourth. The
design matters too: a paired test is more powerful than unpaired when
pairing is valid (this is the accurate version of ``a t-test is best'').
→ Type I and Type II errors, t-tests (one-sample, paired, two-sample).

\hypertarget{biological-vs-technical-replicates}{%
\subsubsection{Biological vs technical
replicates}\label{biological-vs-technical-replicates}}

\textbf{Definition.} \emph{Biological replicates} are independent
samples (e.g.~different mice); \emph{technical replicates} re-measure
the \textbf{same} sample (e.g.~the same mouse assayed three times).

\textbf{Intuition.} Technical replicates only capture \emph{measurement}
noise; biological replicates capture the \emph{biological} variability
you actually want to generalize over. Many mice beat one mouse measured
many times, because one mouse can't tell you about the population.

\textbf{Example.} To claim a drug lowers weight in mice, 20
\emph{different} mice (biological) support a population-level
conclusion; weighing \emph{one} mouse 20 times (technical) only shows
your scale is consistent.

\textbf{Notes.} More biological replicates raise statistical power;
technical replicates mainly reduce assay noise. → Statistical power and
power analysis.

\hypertarget{rpkm-fpkm-and-tpm-rna-seq-normalization}{%
\subsubsection{RPKM, FPKM, and TPM (RNA-seq
normalization)}\label{rpkm-fpkm-and-tpm-rna-seq-normalization}}

\textbf{Definition.} Normalizations that make RNA-seq read counts
comparable across genes and samples. \emph{RPKM} (reads per kilobase per
million) divides counts by gene length (in kilobases) and by sequencing
depth (in millions of reads); \emph{FPKM} is the paired-end version
(fragments, not reads); \emph{TPM} (transcripts per million) applies the
same two corrections in the \textbf{opposite order} so every sample's
values sum to the same total.

\textbf{Intuition.} Longer genes and more-deeply-sequenced samples rack
up more reads for no biological reason; these divide that artefact out.
TPM is preferred because its constant per-sample total makes proportions
comparable across samples, which RPKM/FPKM don't guarantee.

\textbf{Example.} A 4 kb gene with 400 reads in a 10-million-read
sample: \texttt{RPKM\ =\ 400\ /\ (4\ ×\ 10)\ =\ 10}. TPM rescales so the
sample's values sum to 1,000,000.

\textbf{Notes.} Niche (bioinformatics), included for completeness ---
prefer TPM. \emph{(The jotted ``normalizing with columns and first row''
wasn't quite the mechanism; the two divisors are gene length and read
depth.)}

  in \texttt{Y\ =\ f(X)\ +\ ε}. →
  \protect\hyperlink{statistical-learning}{Statistical learning}.
\item
  \textbf{Step functions} --- piecewise-constant fit over bins of
  \texttt{X}. → \protect\hyperlink{step-functions}{Step functions}.
\item
  \textbf{Stepwise selection} --- greedily add (forward) or drop
  (backward) predictors. →
  \protect\hyperlink{forward-and-backward-stepwise-selection}{Forward
  and backward stepwise selection}.
\item
  \textbf{Subset selection (best)} --- try all \texttt{2\^{}p} predictor
  subsets. → \protect\hyperlink{best-subset-selection}{Best subset
  selection}.
\item
  \textbf{Supervised / unsupervised learning} --- labeled \texttt{(x,y)}
  vs predictors-only. →
  \protect\hyperlink{supervised-vs-unsupervised-learning}{Supervised vs
  unsupervised learning}.
\item
  \textbf{Survival analysis (censored data)} --- modeling
  time-until-event with censored (incomplete) observations. →
  \protect\hyperlink{survival-analysis-and-censored-data}{Survival
  analysis and censored data}.
\item
  \textbf{Survival function} ---
  \texttt{S(t)\ =\ Pr(T\ \textgreater{}\ t)}, probability of surviving
  past \texttt{t}. → \protect\hyperlink{survival-function}{Survival
  function}.
\item
  \textbf{t-statistic} --- coefficient estimate in units of its standard
  error. →
  \protect\hyperlink{hypothesis-test-t-statistic-and-p-value}{Hypothesis
  test, t-statistic, and p-value}.
\item
  \textbf{Test / training MSE} --- error on unseen vs fitting data; test
  is U-shaped in flexibility. →
  \protect\hyperlink{training-mse-vs-test-mse}{Training MSE vs test
  MSE}.
\item
  \textbf{t-test} --- compares means via t = mean-difference / its
  standard error. →
  \protect\hyperlink{t-tests-one-sample-paired-two-sample}{t-tests
  (one-sample, paired, two-sample)}.
\item
  \textbf{Type I / Type II error} --- false positive (reject true null)
  vs false negative (miss a real effect). →
  \protect\hyperlink{type-i-and-type-ii-errors}{Type I and Type II
  errors}.
\item
  \textbf{Validation set approach} --- single train/hold-out split. →
  \protect\hyperlink{validation-set-approach}{Validation set approach}.
\item
  \textbf{Variance} --- how much \texttt{} shifts across training sets;
  rises with flexibility. → \protect\hyperlink{variance}{Variance}.
\item
  \textbf{Variable selection} --- choosing which predictors to include.
  → \protect\hyperlink{best-subset-selection}{Best subset selection}.
\item
  \textbf{Z-score (standardization)} --- z = (x − μ)/σ; rescales to mean
  0, SD 1. → \protect\hyperlink{z-scores-standardization}{Z-scores
  (standardization)}.
\end{itemize}
